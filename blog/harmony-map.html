Reading the symbols — four different "7"s that are not the same thing
♭7flat before number
a scale degree — one note. "Major + ♭7" means the 7th note is lowered (F♯→F in G). It describes the scale, not any chord.
1 note
♭VIIflat + Roman
the chord built on that flatted degree — a plain triad unless marked otherwise. In G, ♭VII = F major. Not F7.
F A C
F7number after letter
a chord extension — a minor 7th stacked above that chord's own root. Nothing to do with scale degrees.
F A C E♭
Fmaj7maj7 after letter
same idea, but the natural 7th above the chord's root.
F A C E

The rule: accidental before the number (♭7, ♯4) = scale degree, one note. Number after a chord letter (F7, G7, Cmaj7) = extension stacked on that chord. ♭VII = the triad built on the flatted degree.

A Roman numeral never contains a seventh. It's a position label — where the chord sits, not what's stacked in it. ♭VII is a plain triad; a seventh must be appended explicitly (V7, ♭VII7, ii7). You already use this grammar: in your own 1 · 2m · 3m · 4 · 5 · 6m · 7dim formula, "7dim" means built on the 7th degree — you've never read it as a dominant seventh. ♭VII is the same move in Roman.

Where the ♭7 note actually pays off: not on the ♭VII chord — on the tonic. In G Mixolydian, stack a 7th on home using the scale's notes: G–B–D–F = G7. The flat-7 degree turns your I chord into a dominant 7 — that unresolved G7-as-home is the bluesy sound. Meanwhile the ♭VII stays plain F (its own 7th would be E, giving Fmaj7, not F7).

The two moves in your formulas: "make it major" = raise the 3rd · "make it augmented (aug)" = raise the 5th. That's exactly what harmonic minor does — 3m→3 raises a 3rd, 5→5aug raises a 5th.

Naming a big extension implies the ones below it. "C13" technically stacks 7–9–11–13, but in practice you play root, 3rd, 7th + the named note and drop whatever muddies it (the 11 especially clashes over a major 3rd). The number just tells you the highest note reached.

How to read any ▲ — the slot rule

One altered note changes every chord containing it — but how each chord changes depends on which slot of the triad that note occupies. Worked example, G Mixolydian's one change (F♯ → F), hitting three chords three different ways:

F♯dim → F
F♯ was the root (F♯–A–C). Lower the bottom note and both intervals above it stretch by a semitone: minor 3rd → major 3rd, tritone → perfect 5th. Both diminished ingredients repaired at once — dim jumps all the way to major.
D → Dm
F♯ was the 3rd (D–F♯–A). Lower the middle note and only that interval shrinks: major 3rd → minor 3rd. Major becomes minor.
Bm → Bdim
F♯ was the 5th (B–D–F♯). Lower the top note: perfect 5th → tritone. Minor becomes diminished.

The slot rule: alter the root → both intervals move (biggest quality jump). Alter the 3rd or 5th → one interval moves. Every scale note lives in exactly three triads (as root, as 3rd, as 5th), so one note-change always produces exactly three ▲s — each transformed according to its slot. To decode any ▲ in the tables above: find which chord tone the altered note is, and the quality change follows mechanically.

And why "♭7" at all? Mixolydian doesn't flatten anything. It's born as a rotation — major scale, home moved to degree 5, zero notes changed. Measured against the major scale of the new root, the 7th merely lands a whole step below home. Every mode formula (♭3 ♭7, ♭2 ♭6 ♭7…) is a comparison, not a construction: the shadow the rotation casts against plain major. Parent frame: "nothing altered, home moved." Same-root frame: "major, bent." Same object, two frames — this explorer shows the same-root frame; §10's mode ladder shows the rotation frame. Same modes, two true pictures.

Each mode is shown as the major scale of the chosen root, bent into the mode — the top row is plain major, the middle row is the mode, and ▲ marks every chord that changed. This is the honest picture: e.g. Phrygian Dominant's III really is a diminished chord (it's in the scale), while its Andalusian cadence borrows a ♭III major that isn't — flagged separately under "borrowed." Inventory (what's in the scale) and progression (what you play) are different things, and both are shown so they never look like a contradiction.

The rotation principle

Rotation only works on loops with no directed resolution. A resolution pins home — rotate it and it dies. A resolution-free loop leaves home up for grabs — whichever chord comes first, the ear crowns it. Both halves are playable below; the precise rule is at the bottom.

Key: C
Half one — the axis rotates: same four chords, four homes
Half two — a cadence doesn't: rotate it and home dissolves
What "resolution" precisely means — the delivery test

Home is pinned whenever a half-step pull is delivered onto an arrival chord. It doesn't matter which chord carries the pull — only whether it lands. "Resolution" is not "contains a V," and avoiding V→I is not enough to escape it. The three carriers:

V → I
the leading tone (a half-step below home) pulls up into the tonic — the classical discharge from §01.
vii° → I
the same leading tone plus the tritone — §03's chord, an even more desperate version of the same pull.
♭II → I
a half-step drop from above — the Andalusian's landing. No dominant anywhere, and it pins home just as hard.

Run the test on the three cases you can play in this guide:

Axis
the pull exists but is dodged — V discharges onto vi every pass, never I; the only arrival at home is a soft plagal IV→I with no half-step pull. Nothing lands → home up for grabs → rotates.
Andalusian
no V→I anywhere, but ♭II lands square on I at the end of a directed descent. Delivered → home pinned → rotation kills it.
Mixo vamp
G ⇄ F: no half-step root motion at all — nothing to deliver. That's why it can vamp forever.

The trap this kills: "no resolution = V never goes to I." Check that rule against the Andalusian — it says the Andalusian should rotate fine (no V→I present). Your ears, two blocks up, say it dies. The ears carry the correct definition: find the half-step pulls, ask whether the shape delivers them onto a landing. Delivered = pinned. Dodged or absent = loop.

The two-condition test for whether a rotation is a real idiom: (1) the loop can rotate — nothing in it strongly resolves, so any chord can be heard as home; (2) repertoire has rotated it — songs actually exist on that rotation. The axis passes both on three of its four faces; the V rotation is the weakest, because opening on V reawakens its pull toward I and sneaks function back in. And the Andalusian fails condition (1) outright: its identity is the landing, so the rotation above isn't a variant — it's rubble.

Why this is the same principle as everything else here: the relative-minor flip (§07), the mode ladder (§10), the axis verse/chorus contrast, and the bridge devices are all one move — keep the inventory, relocate home. Rotation is that move applied to a four-chord loop. Function is the thing that resists it; modality and the axis are what harmony sounds like when function lets go.

01 — THE ENGINE

Why the dominant wants to resolve to the tonic

The dominant chord (V) is loaded with tendency tones that pull the ear toward the stability of home base. The V–I cadence is the essential cycle of tension and release.

The leading tone effect

The 3rd of the dominant is the 7th scale degree — B in the key of C. Because it sits a single half-step below the tonic root, it feels unfinished and screams to resolve upward into the 1.

The tritone clash

In a dominant 7th chord (V⁷), the 3rd and 7th form a tritone — B and F inside a G⁷. That interval is highly dissonant and craves resolution, forcing the notes outward or inward in a satisfying pincer motion onto the consonant tonic.

Cultural conditioning

Centuries of Western tradition have wired the journey from tension to release into our ears, so the resolution simply feels natural and complete.

The one-line memory hook: the dominant contains the two most restless notes in the key — the leading tone and the tritone — and both point straight home.
02 — THE VOCABULARY

What "diatonic" actually means

Not "true to the tonic." It means across the tones — staying strictly within the seven notes of one key's signature, with zero outside sharps or flats.

The word is Greek: dia (through / across) + tonos (tone). It describes travelling through the natural notes of a scale.

The in-key rule

In C major the diatonic notes are strictly C D E F G A B. Because there are no accidentals, even B diminished (B–D–F) is fully diatonic.

The out-of-key rule

Change that B to a B♭ to make a B♭ major chord and you have left the key. That chord is chromatic (non-diatonic), because B♭ isn't part of the C major scale.

Tonic vs diatonic: the tonic is the single home note or chord — the destination. Diatonic is the whole seven-chord family that respects the key's rules. One is a place; the other is a citizenship test.
03 — THE CLASSIC MIX-UP

The 7th chord trap: vii° is not the subtonic

In the major sequence 1 · 2m · 3m · 4 · 5 · 6m · 7dim, that final chord is the leading-tone diminished chord (vii°) — not the subtonic. Both live near the 7th degree, but they use different notes and pull in opposite directions.

vii° — the leading-tone diminished

Built on the natural 7th (B in C). Sits exactly a half-step below the tonic. Highly tense and dramatic — it shares three notes with the dominant 7th, so it acts like a dominant and desperately resolves up to the 1.

♭VII — the subtonic

Built on the lowered 7th (B♭ in C). Sits a whole-step below the tonic. Epic, rock-and-roll, cinematic. It does not occur in the standard major scale — it's borrowed from Mixolydian or the minor scale.

The naming rule: "subtonic" is reserved exclusively for the flat-7 (♭7) and its chord (♭VII). The natural 7th of a major scale is always the leading tone.
04 — THE EMOTIONAL ROLES

The job every diatonic chord plays

Beyond the tonic and dominant, each chord carries a distinct narrative feel. Here's the cast, by scale degree in C major.

IV
Subdominant · F
Hopeful, expansive, open. A gentle lift away from home without heavy tension — like beginning a journey or looking up at a wide sky.
♭VII
Subtonic · B♭ (borrowed)
Epic, cinematic, rebellious. Strips out classical tension and swaps in a heroic, modal, adventurous quality. The engine of rock.
vi
Submediant · relative minor of I · Am
Bittersweet, nostalgic, vulnerable. Shares two notes with the tonic, so it feels like home with a sudden shadow. The classic "emotional twist" chord.
ii
Supertonic · relative minor of IV · Dm
Melancholic, yearning, preparatory. The jazz and pop-ballad workhorse — a weeping momentum that signals a larger shift is coming.
iii
Mediant · relative minor of V · Em
Dreamy, static, introspective. The rarest of the primary minors. A floating sensation — a daydream, a memory, time briefly suspended.
05 — LEAVING THE KEY

The four neighborhoods of chromaticism

Step outside the seven-chord town and you're in chromatic territory — any of the 12 notes, chosen for drama, spice, and cinematic color. Songwriters get there four main ways.

Modal MixtureThe Borrowers
Nostalgic · epic

Steal a chord from the parallel key — same home note, opposite quality. If you're in C major, you borrow from C minor. Home never moves; you just recolor one chord.

Parallel — this is the one
Same root, flip the quality

C major ↔ C minor. Both call C "home." You reach into C minor and lift out a chord.

same 1 · different notes
Relative — a different tool
Same notes, move the home

C major ↔ A minor. Identical seven notes, but home shifts to A. This is the §07 minor flip, not borrowing.

same notes · different 1

So in C major, borrowing from C minor (Cm · D° · E♭ · Fm · Gm · A♭ · B♭) gives you:

♭III
E♭ major — a bright, unexpected lift.
iv
F minor — the ultimate Hollywood-heartbreak chord.
♭VI
A♭ major — built on the flatted 6th (A♭). Normally the 6 chord is A minor; mixture swaps in A♭ major. Heroic, spacey.
♭VII
B♭ major — the borrowed flat-7 subtonic, that driving rock feel.
The trap: ♭VI is A♭ major, not "Cm as 6m." Cm is the 6m of E♭ — a relative relationship, which moves the home. Modal mixture keeps home on C and only borrows the chord.
Secondary DominantsThe Gravity Boosters
Sophisticated · theatrical · directional

Temporarily treat a non-tonic chord as "home" and slam into it with its dominant. Secondary leading-tone (diminished) chords do the same job with a darker, creeping tension.

C → A7 → Dm (A7 is the V of Dm)
Chromatic MediantsThe Teleporters
Sudden shift in color, space, dimension

Jump to a chord a third away but keep it major or minor against the key — a portal effect used constantly in sci-fi and fantasy scores.

C major → E major (instead of the diatonic Em)
Symmetrical & Special ChordsThe Gravity-Defiers
Chaos · suspense · total loss of gravity

Built on math, not scale steps, so they have no home. Fully diminished 7th — a stack of minor thirds; perfectly symmetrical, resolves to four keys, a musical wormhole. Whole-tone chords — the flashback / underwater float. Plus the Neapolitan (♭II): a major chord on the flat-2 (D♭ in C), tragic and operatic, dropping heavily to the dominant.

The overarching split: functional chromaticism has a job — it points your ear at a target chord (secondary dominants). Non-functional "color" chromaticism is there purely for texture and doesn't resolve where you expect.

What changes when the home moves

The home (i) is now minor — Am — heavy, serious, sad. The subtonic (♭VII) is now fully diatonic: G major, sitting a whole-step below the new home, is genuinely in the family here. And the magnet breaks: the 5 chord is now Em (minor). Lacking a sharp leading tone — G natural instead of G♯ — it barely pulls you home.

08 — THE HYBRID

Harmonic minor: the border town

Composers hated the weak minor v. The fix: manually sharpen the 7th (G → G♯), turning Em back into E major. That single note re-imports the dominant-to-tonic engine into the dark minor world — a hybrid sitting right on the diatonic/chromatic line.

The one-note formula shift (in A minor)

Natural minor: Am · Bdim · C · Dm · Em · F · G
Harmonic minor: Am · Bdim · Caug · Dm · E · F · G♯dim

You gain a driving major V and a tense vii° diminished that force the music violently back home. The cost: a three-half-step gap between the 6th and 7th degrees — the signature exotic sound of classical drama, gothic themes, metal, and flamenco.

09 — LOCKED TO THE MAJOR NUMBERS

The minor cheats, in your own format

Keep the numbers welded to the major scale and just move your starting line to the 6m. Now every "cheat" is visible as a single altered chord in the family tree — you can see exactly what got bent.

■ home base (6m) ■ altered chord ■ untouched diatonic chord
Pure Natural MinorThe baseline
6m–7dim–1–2m–3m–4–5

The problem: the jump from 5 → 6m feels weak. The 5 chord is minor here, so it has no sharp leading tone to lock into home.

Harmonic Minor CheatFixing the 5
6m–7dim–1–2m–3–4–5aug

The one raised note (a half-step below home) sits inside the 3, the 5 and the 7. It turns 3m → 3 (major) and 5 → 5aug. Now 3 → 6m is a true dominant-to-tonic slam inside the minor loop. The 3 major is your working V-of-home; the leading-tone diminished lands on the 7.

Melodic Minor CheatSmoothing the climb
6m–7dim–1–2–3–4–5aug

Harmonic minor leaves a jagged three-half-step gap. Melodic minor raises a second note (a whole step below home) to smooth the run up to the root — that turns 2m → 2 (major) as well. You get a bright, stepwise ascent with the dominant pull intact.

Dorian Rock VariantThe Santana / Floyd vibe
6m–7dim–1–2–3m–4–5

Want groovy and heroic instead of sad? Raise just the 2m → 2 (major). Looping 6m → 2 gives that bright, spacey minor lift heard all over rock jams.

Correction from the source: harmonic minor's altered chords are 3 (major) and 5aug — never a "5♯dim." The diminished chord is the vii° on the 7, and the raised dominant is augmented, not diminished. Two different chords, easy to conflate.

Locrianhome · 7dimunstable, rarely a home key

The darkest — home is a diminished chord, so it never rests. Almost no songs live here; it's mostly a passing color or a jazz-theory curiosity.

The pattern to lock in: read the ladder as one loop of the major scale with a moving home. Everything from Lydian to Locrian is the same seven chords — the only variable is where you decide "1" is.
