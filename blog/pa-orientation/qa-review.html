Urinary retention
presentation · 24 questions · 7 groups 2 to confirm
When patients ask…
Understanding what’s wrong

Why can’t I pee — my bladder feels like it’s bursting?

Your bladder’s full and stretched, but the urine can’t get out — either because the outlet is blocked, often by an enlarged prostate or a narrowing, or because the bladder muscle isn’t squeezing as it should. That trapped, stretched feeling is exactly why it’s so uncomfortable, and draining it is the first thing we’ll do.

Why: Lead with the mechanism they can feel (full + can’t empty) and split it into the two source-buckets — outlet block vs bladder-not-contracting — without committing to a cause before the scan.

Is it my prostate — could this be cancer?

It’s a fair thing to ask and I won’t brush it aside. In older men an enlarged prostate is the commonest reason for this, and that enlargement is usually benign rather than a cancer — but because we can’t tell size and cancer apart by the block alone, we’ll examine the prostate and check it out properly rather than just assume. We’re not ignoring the worry; we’ll look into it.

Why: Never a flat “no” to cancer. Validate, name BPH as the usual benign cause from the differential, but be honest that retention itself doesn’t distinguish benign from malignant — the workup (DRE) does.

Why has this happened now, all of a sudden?

Often things have been narrowing slowly for a while — a weaker stream or getting up in the night — and then something tips it over the edge so nothing will come at all. Once we’ve relieved it, we’ll work out what the underlying cause is rather than just leaving it there.

Is this something I did to bring it on?

No — this isn’t about anything you did wrong. It’s usually the plumbing gradually narrowing or the bladder tiring, not a mistake on your part. Some things like certain cold or allergy medicines can occasionally tip a borderline bladder over, and we’ll go through your tablets, but you’re not to blame for this.

The symptom

Am I going to need a catheter? I really don’t want one.

I know the idea of it puts people off — but honestly it’s the quickest way to stop this pain, and most people say the relief once it’s draining far outweighs the moment of putting it in. It’s a soft tube passed up the natural passage, we use plenty of numbing gel, and for a lot of people it’s only in until we’ve sorted the cause.

Why: The catheter is the dreaded thing — name the dread, validate it, then reframe around the relief. Don’t over-promise it’ll be brief for everyone (chronic retention may need it longer); “for a lot of people” keeps it honest.

A bit still dribbles out — so I’m not completely blocked, am I?

Those few dribbles are actually overflow — the bladder’s so full that a little leaks past the blockage, which can fool you into thinking you’re emptying. It doesn’t mean you’re draining properly, so we still treat this as a full, blocked bladder that needs relieving.

How long can it be left like this before it does harm?

It’s not something to sit on — a tense, full bladder is painful, can get infected, and the back-pressure can start to strain the kidneys if it’s left. That’s why we relieve it promptly rather than waiting, and then look at why it happened.

Why: Anchors the urgency to the redFlags (infection, back-pressure on kidneys) so “just wait it out” isn’t on the table, without frightening them about a timeline the source doesn’t specify.
What happens now

What are you going to do first?

First we scan to confirm how much is trapped, then we pass a catheter to drain it — that’s what takes the pain away. We measure what comes out, test the urine, and do some blood tests, and then we work out and treat the cause rather than just leaving the tube in.

Will I be stuck with a catheter forever — is this permanent?

For most people, no. The catheter is a bridge to get you comfortable and buy time, not the long-term answer — the plan is to treat what caused the block so you can eventually pass urine yourself. Some people do need it a while, or occasionally a trial without it later on, and we’ll be straight with you about which path looks likely for you.

Why: Directly answers the “is it permanent” dread. Reassure honestly (catheter is a bridge, from management) but don’t promise everyone regains function — flag TWOC/longer-term possibility so it isn’t a broken promise.

What if the tube won’t go in?

Occasionally the usual route is too narrow — say from an old narrowing — and the tube won’t pass. If that happens there’s another safe way to drain the bladder through a small tube just above the pubic bone instead, so we can always relieve it one way or another.

Why: Covers the suprapubic fallback from management (impassable stricture) reassuringly — the point is “we can always drain you,” not the technicalities.

How do you actually fix the cause so it doesn’t just happen again?

That depends on what we find. If it’s an enlarged prostate, there are medicines that relax or shrink it, and an operation if those aren’t enough; if it’s a narrowing of the passage, that can be repaired surgically. The catheter buys us the time to sort the right one for you.

Does this mean I’ll need an operation?

Not necessarily — a lot of causes, like an enlarged prostate, are treated with tablets first, and surgery is only if those don’t do the job or the cause needs it, such as repairing a narrowing. We’ll talk you through whether an operation is likely for your situation, and the surgeon would go through the details and consent with you.

Why: Respect scope — formal consent and operative detail are the operating surgeon’s job, not the PA’s; keep this to setting expectations.
Day to day

Now the tube’s in I’m peeing huge amounts — is something wrong?

That can actually be expected after we relieve a bladder that’s been backed up for a while — the kidneys briefly overshoot and pour out a lot of urine while things rebalance. It’s not a sign it’s gone wrong, but because you can lose a lot of fluid and salts, we keep a close eye on your output, top up your fluids, and check your blood tests until it settles.

Why: Post-obstructive diuresis — straight from redFlags/management/pearls. Reassure it’s expected, but state plainly we monitor fluids and electrolytes so it doesn’t read as “ignore it.”

How do I manage day to day if I go home with the catheter?review

Plenty of people go home with one for a spell and get on fine — you’ll have a bag, we’ll show you and anyone helping you how to look after it and keep it clean, and a home-care nurse, arranged through Ontario Health atHome, can support you. We’ll make sure you leave knowing what’s normal and what isn’t before you go.

Should I drink less so my bladder doesn’t fill up?review

No — don’t cut your fluids down. With the catheter draining, the urine has somewhere to go, and drinking normally helps keep the bladder flushed and lowers the infection risk. If anything, keeping well hydrated is what we’d want.

When to come straight back

When should I worry — when do I come straight back?

Come straight back if you get a fever or the shivers, if you’re passing blood or clots in your urine, if the catheter stops draining and the pain and swelling build up again, or if you feel very unwell in yourself. Any of those need to be seen urgently, not left overnight.

Why: The safety-net is the single most important thing they leave with — built from the redFlags (infection/urosepsis, clot retention, obstruction). Say it plainly, every time.

What are the really serious signs I mustn’t ignore?

The ones that need emergency attention are new numbness around your back passage or between your legs, difficulty controlling your bowels, or new weakness in your legs. Those can point to pressure on the nerves and must be checked the same day — please don’t sit on them.

Why: Cauda equina, from redFlags — painless retention can be its only urological clue. High-stakes and time-critical, so it earns its own plain-language safety-net item; “same day” conveys urgency without diagnosing.

There’s blood coming down the tube — is that an emergency?

A little pink is common after a catheter goes in, but heavier bleeding or passing clots isn’t something to wait on — clots can block the tube and let the bladder fill up painfully again. Tell us straight away if you see clots or the flow slows down or stops.

Why: Clot retention from redFlags — distinguish the common minor pink tinge from clots/heavy bleeding that block drainage, so they know which one to act on.
Outlook

Once this is sorted, will it happen again?

It can, especially if the underlying cause isn’t fully treated — which is exactly why we don’t just drain it and leave it, but go after the reason. Treating the cause well is what gives you the best chance of not landing back here, and we’ll set that plan up with you.

Why: Don’t falsely reassure — recurrence is real if the cause is left. Pivot to “that’s why we treat the cause,” echoing the management principle that the catheter is a bridge, not the cure.

Has this done any lasting damage to my kidneys?

In many people, once we relieve the pressure the kidneys recover well. Because the back-up can strain them, we check your kidney blood tests and sometimes scan them, and that tells us whether they’ve been affected and need closer follow-up. We’ll share those results with you rather than leave you guessing.

Why: Rising creatinine / bilateral hydronephrosis from redFlags and workup. Honest — usually recoverable but we check — rather than a blanket “you’re fine.”
Tests & scansshared

Does the scan hurt?

No — it’s painless. An ultrasound is a bit of cool gel and a probe pressed gently on your skin. A CT is a few minutes lying still while the table slides through a ring. An MRI means lying still in a tunnel-like scanner for about half an hour or more, and it’s noisy, so you get ear protection. We’ll tell you which one you’re having.

Is the radiation dangerous?

Ultrasound and MRI use no radiation at all. A CT scan does, but the dose is small, and we only do one when what it tells us is worth it. For the odd scan the risk is tiny next to the benefit of getting the diagnosis right.

Why: Answer the proportionality honestly rather than “it’s totally safe” — patients trust a real risk-benefit more than a brush-off.

The dye — is that safe, especially for my kidneys?

For most people it’s very safe. We check a kidney blood test first, and you flush it out afterwards with normal drinking. Do tell us about any past reaction to contrast or bad allergies.

Why: Name the two real caveats — renal function and prior reaction — so it reads as careful, not dismissive.

When will I get the results?

Some results we can tell you on the day, but a full scan report usually takes a few days. Either way, someone will talk you through what it shows before any decisions are made — you won’t be left guessing.

Visible haematuria
presentation · 24 questions · 7 groups 2 to confirm
When patients ask…
Understanding what’s wrong

What does it mean that there’s blood in my urine?

It means blood is getting into your urine somewhere along the urinary tract — the kidneys, the tubes down from them, the bladder, or the outlet. Seeing it, or finding it on a dip test, is the signal to go looking for where it’s coming from. The blood itself isn’t the illness — it’s the clue that points us to the cause.

It looks like a lot of blood — have I lost a dangerous amount?

It usually looks far more dramatic than it is — even a tiny amount of blood turns a whole toilet-bowl red, so please don’t judge it by the colour. We’ll check a blood count to be sure. What matters more than the colour is whether it’s still flowing heavily or you’re passing clots, and we’ll keep an eye on that.

Why: Patients equate red water with catastrophic loss. Reframe the colour, but don’t dismiss — heavy ongoing bleeding is a genuine red flag, so name what actually worries us.

If it doesn’t even hurt, can it really be a problem?

This is the part people find hardest to believe — painless blood is actually the kind we take most seriously, not the least. Pain would point us toward a stone or infection; blood with no pain at all is the pattern that always earns the full set of tests, precisely because it can be the only warning sign of something that needs catching early.

Why: The core teaching point of this presentation: painless visible haematuria is malignancy-until-proven-otherwise. Counter the intuitive “no pain = no problem” head-on without frightening.
Is it serious

Could this be cancer?

I’m not going to brush that worry off, because it’s the right question to ask. The honest answer is that blood in the urine is taken seriously precisely because it can sometimes be the first sign of a cancer of the bladder or kidney — which is exactly why it always earns a full, proper look rather than a quick reassurance. Most people who bleed turn out not to have a cancer. But the way we reassure you is by checking thoroughly, not by guessing.

Why: Never a flat “no” for painless visible haematuria. The honest line: it’s taken seriously BECAUSE it can signal cancer, so it earns a full workup — reassure via the thoroughness of the look, not by dismissing the fear.

It’s stopped now — do I still need all these tests?

Yes — and I know that’s frustrating when you feel back to normal. Blood in the urine very often settles on its own, but settling doesn’t tell us why it happened, and the causes we most want to rule out don’t announce themselves by carrying on bleeding. So one episode, even a single one that’s gone, is still worth the full check.

Why: Source: “it always earns a full workup even if it settles.” Patients disengage once the visible sign is gone — hold the line that stopping ≠ explained.

Couldn’t it just be a bladder infection?

It might be — but I don’t want to settle on that too quickly, because “probably an infection” is one of the commonest ways a more serious cause gets missed. If a urine sample proves an infection, we treat it, and then we re-test to make sure the blood has completely cleared. If it hasn’t, we keep looking. An infection has to be proven and then seen to resolve — not just assumed.

Why: Source pearl: never close the loop on “probable UTI” unless the culture is positive AND the haematuria clears on a follow-up dip. This is the key safety message of the entry.

I’m on blood thinners — isn’t that just the cause?review

It’s a fair thought, and we’ll always review your blood thinners and check your clotting as part of this. But here’s the important bit: being on a thinner doesn’t excuse us from looking for a cause. It can make a bleed show up, but it often makes visible something that was there to be found — so we still do the same full check rather than blaming the tablets and stopping there.

Why: Source only lists “clotting/anticoagulant review” in workup — it does NOT state anticoagulants are a sufficient explanation. Standard teaching is that anticoagulation unmasks rather than fully explains haematuria; keep the answer to that safe general line and flag for clinician confirmation.
The tests

What tests will I actually have?

Three main things. A urine sample to test and culture it. A scan called a CT urogram that pictures your kidneys and the tubes — the upper part of the system. And a cystoscopy, where we look inside the bladder itself with a small camera. Together those cover the whole tract, top to bottom.

If the scan’s clear, why do I still need a camera in the bladder?

Because the scan and the camera look at different places, and one can’t stand in for the other. The CT urogram is very good at the kidneys and the tubes, but it’s the weakest at seeing the lining of the bladder — and that’s exactly where the camera is best. So a clear scan is genuinely good news for the top half, but it doesn’t clear the bladder. We need both to say we’ve properly looked.

Why: Source: “together CT urogram + cystoscopy form the complete workup … neither alone is enough to clear a patient.” Patients read a clear CT as the all-clear and decline the scope — explain the complementary blind spots.

What is a cystoscopy?

It’s a way of looking directly inside your bladder. A thin, flexible tube with a tiny camera on the end passes along the natural water pipe into the bladder, and we inspect the lining as we go. It’s the most reliable way to check the one place the scan can’t see well.

Does the cystoscopy hurt?

It’s uncomfortable rather than painful for most people — a stinging or a strong urge to pee as the camera goes in and the bladder fills. We use a numbing, lubricating gel first to take the edge off, and it’s usually over in a few minutes. It’s common to sting a little when you pee for a day or so afterwards, and to see a pink tinge — that settles.

Why: The most-feared test in this set. Be honest (“uncomfortable, not painful”), name the gel and the short duration, and pre-warn the transient stinging/pink urine so they don’t panic and think something’s wrong.

Will I be awake for the camera test?

The flexible camera check is usually done while you’re awake, with just the numbing gel — you can be up and home soon after. Whoever’s arranging yours will confirm exactly how it’s being done for you.

Why: OVERRIDES the shared anaesthetic “asleep?” expectation — flexible cystoscopy is typically an awake, local-gel outpatient procedure, unlike the general-anaesthetic default.
What happens now

Why am I being referred so urgently?

Not because we’ve found something bad — we haven’t looked yet. It’s because blood in the urine is one of the things we don’t sit on. Being seen quickly is how we make sure that if there is a cause worth catching early, we catch it early. Fast doesn’t mean bad news; it means we’re taking it seriously.

What if they do find something?

Then we’ll know exactly what we’re dealing with, and there’ll be a clear plan — the cause gets treated on its own terms. A stone, an infection, an inflamed lining and the more serious causes are all handled differently, so the first job is simply to find out which it is. I’d rather not guess at treatment before we know.

And if everything comes back clear?

That’s the outcome we see most often, and it’s genuinely reassuring — it means both the scan and the camera have had a proper look and found nothing worrying. Sometimes the bleeding just settles and no single cause is pinned down. We’ll tell you clearly if anything needs keeping an eye on, or if you can draw a line under it.

Why: Give the honest “often nothing is found” reassurance the cancer-fear patient needs — but only after both tests, mirroring the “neither alone clears you” rule.
Day to day

Can I carry on as normal while I wait?

For the most part, yes — carry on with your usual day. The main things to watch for are the warning signs I’ll go through with you: heavy bleeding, or trouble peeing. Short of those, there’s no need to tiptoe around while the tests are arranged.

Should I drink more water?

Drinking normally is sensible and helps keep things flushing through, especially if you’re passing the odd small clot. There’s no need to force huge amounts. If you’re bleeding more heavily, staying well hydrated is worth it — but that situation needs us to know about it too.

Why: Generic-safe (source doesn’t prescribe fluids for haematuria). Frame as sensible general advice, and route heavier bleeding back to review rather than self-managing with water.
When to come straight back

What if I suddenly can’t pee?

Come straight back — that’s one we don’t wait on. If clots block the outlet, the bladder fills up, can’t empty, and becomes painful and swollen. That needs sorting urgently with a special catheter that flushes the clots out — so don’t sit at home hoping it passes. Being unable to pee at all, with a full, painful belly, is an emergency.

Why: Red flag from source: clot retention — clots plug the outlet, painful retention needing a three-way catheter and irrigation, “not reassurance.” Must be stated plainly, every time.

When is the bleeding itself an emergency?

If it’s heavy and not letting up, or you’re passing a lot of clots, or you feel dizzy, faint, clammy or your heart’s racing — come straight back or call for help. Those are signs you may be losing more blood than is safe, and that needs seeing urgently rather than waiting for your appointment.

Why: Red flag from source: haemodynamic compromise from heavy bleeding — tachycardia and falling haemoglobin mean resuscitate/transfuse. Translate into plain lay warning signs (dizzy, faint, racing heart).

What about a fever?review

A fever or the shivers along with the bleeding, or if peeing becomes very painful and you feel unwell, is worth getting checked promptly — it can mean an infection that needs treating. When in doubt, it’s always better to be seen.

Why: Not an explicit red flag in the source (which lists clot retention and heavy bleeding). Kept as a safely generic “get seen” safety-net; flagged so the reviewer can confirm whether to keep, drop, or sharpen it for this presentation.

Acute scrotum
presentation · 26 questions · 9 groups
When patients ask…
What’s happening

What’s actually causing this pain?

Sudden, severe pain in a testicle — often with swelling and feeling sick to your stomach — usually means one of two things. The one we most need to rule out is a twist: the testicle can turn on the cord that feeds it, which chokes off its blood supply. The other common cause is an infection. Until we’ve checked, we treat it as the twist, because that’s the one where every minute counts.

What do you mean it’s “twisted”?

The testicle hangs on a cord that carries its blood. In some men that cord can rotate, kinking off the blood the way a kinked hose stops water. That’s why it comes on so fast and hurts so much — and it’s why we don’t sit and wait on it.

Why: The hose image lands the mechanism and the urgency in one go, without jargon.

Could this be something serious, like cancer?

It’s a natural thing to worry about and I won’t brush it off. Sudden pain like this is much more likely a twist or an infection — cancer in a testicle usually shows up as a painless lump, though now and then it does cause pain. Right now our whole focus is protecting the blood supply, and the exam and any scan look at the whole testicle. If anything needs a second look later, we’ll follow it up properly — we won’t leave it hanging.

Why: Mirror the renal_colic cancer template: validate, say this isn’t cancer, but promise honest follow-up rather than a flat “no.”
Why the rush

Why do I need surgery so fast — can’t we wait and see?

If this is a twist, the testicle only survives while its blood is getting through. There’s roughly a six-hour window from when the pain started before it’s at serious risk, and the odds of saving it drop steadily after that. So the rush isn’t us being dramatic — waiting is the one thing that can cost you the testicle. That’s why we act on a strong suspicion rather than waiting for certainty.

Why: The six-hour window is the single most load-bearing fact in this whole set — state it plainly and tie it directly to saving the testis.

Can’t you just scan it first to be sure?

Sometimes we do a quick ultrasound if it can be done without holding anything up — but a scan that looks normal doesn’t rule a twist out, and waiting for one can burn the very time we’re racing against. So if the story and the examination point to a twist, we go to the operating room rather than let a scan delay us. It’s safer to look and find nothing than to wait and lose the testicle.

Why: The entry is emphatic that a normal/equivocal Doppler must never override a convincing history — say this plainly so a parent who wants “just a scan first” understands the trade-off.

And if it turns out it wasn’t a twist?

That happens, and it’s not a failure — it’s the safe outcome. If the pain is actually an infection, we treat that with antibiotics, pain relief and a bit of support for the area. The rule we work to is simple: when we genuinely can’t be sure, we treat it as a twist and look. A normal operation is a far better result than a testicle lost to waiting.

Why: Names the honest trade-off from the entry’s governing rule — a negative exploration beats a lost testis — in patient-friendly terms.
The operation

What does the operation involve?

It’s called a scrotal exploration. The surgeon makes a small opening, checks the testicle, and if it’s twisted they untwist it and stitch it in place so it can’t twist again. Because the tendency to twist usually affects both sides, they normally stitch the other testicle too, as a one-off safeguard.

Why: The entry specifies bilateral orchidopexy — explain the “both sides” bit up front so it isn’t a surprise on waking.

Why touch the good side if only one twisted?

The shape that let one testicle twist is usually there on both sides, so fixing the healthy one too means it can’t happen to that side in future. It’s a small extra step at the same time, and it saves you ever going through this again on the other side.

Will I be awake or asleep for it?

Asleep — this is done under a general anaesthetic, so you won’t feel or remember any of it. Because it’s an emergency, the team will move quickly to get you ready.

Why: OVERRIDES the shared anaesthetic answer: emergency scrotal exploration is a general, and the emergency timing is condition-specific.

What are the risks of the operation?

Mostly minor and short-lived — soreness, swelling and bruising of the scrotum for a while, and a small wound that heals over. Less commonly there can be a wound infection or some bleeding. The bigger honest point is that if the testicle has been starved of blood for too long it may not survive even after untwisting — that’s about how much time had already passed, not about the surgery. The surgeon will go through all of this and take your consent before anything happens.

Why: Structure it common→less-common→the honest time-dependent one, and keep consent with the surgeon, exactly as the renal_colic risks item does.
Will I lose the testicle

Am I going to lose the testicle?

I can’t promise you won’t, and I won’t pretend otherwise — it honestly depends on how long the blood supply was cut off before we get in there. Getting to the operating room quickly gives it the best possible chance, which is exactly why we’re moving fast. If, when the surgeon looks, it hasn’t survived, the safest thing is to remove it — but that decision is made in the moment, with the testicle in front of them.

Why: The brief is explicit: don’t over-promise the testis can be saved — be honest it depends on time. This is the emotional core of the set.

If I lose one, what does life look like with just one?

A single healthy testicle does the job of two — it makes enough hormone and enough sperm that most men with one notice no difference in their sex life or their health. If a testicle is removed, some men choose to have an artificial one put in later purely for how it looks and feels, and that can be discussed with you in good time.

Why: review: reassurance on single-testicle function and prosthesis option is safe general knowledge, but confirm the site is comfortable stating it and check local practice on prosthesis counselling/timing.

Will it still look and feel normal down there?

If the testicle is saved, it settles back to looking and feeling much as before once the swelling and bruising go. If one has to be removed, that side will feel emptier, and — as above — an artificial one can be considered later if you’d want it. The small scar fades with time.

Fertility & the future

Will this affect my fertility, and can I still have children?

For most men, no — one healthy testicle is enough to father children normally. Even if the affected one is lost, the other usually carries on making sperm as before, so having children later is typically unaffected. It’s a fair thing to ask about, and if you ever have concerns down the line there are simple tests that can check.

Why: review: fertility and future children with a single functioning testis is safe standard knowledge, but the entry gives no fertility data — keep it general and flag for a clinician to confirm the framing and any local sperm-check pathway.

Could the other testicle twist too one day?

That’s exactly why the surgeon usually stitches the other side in place during the same operation — to take that worry off the table. Once it’s fixed, it’s very unlikely to twist — but if you ever get sudden testicle pain again, still treat it as an emergency.

Day to day after

How long until I’m back to normal?

Most people are up and about within a few days, with the soreness easing over a week or two. Supportive underwear helps, and simple painkillers usually cover the discomfort. Wounds and bruising take a little longer to fully settle — we’ll give you a clear idea before you leave.

Why: review: recovery timings are typical general guidance; confirm the site is happy with the range and add any local post-op instructions.

When can I get back to work, sport and driving?

Light activity and desk work usually come back within days, once you’re comfortable. Give heavy lifting, sport and anything that jolts the area a couple of weeks so the repair settles, and only drive once you can do an emergency stop without pain. The team looking after you will tailor this to you.

When is it okay to have sex again?

Once things are comfortable and the wound has settled — usually a couple of weeks — there’s no problem resuming. Let discomfort be your guide, and there’s no rush.

Why: review: generic post-op advice with no clinical detail in the entry — safe to keep vague; a clinician should confirm timing.
When to come straight back

What if the pain comes back, or something feels wrong afterwards?

Come straight back if the pain returns or gets worse, if the scrotum swells up hard, goes very red or hot, if the wound opens or oozes, or if you get a fever or the shivers. And — before any surgery — this is the golden rule for testicle pain generally: any sudden, severe testicle pain is an emergency and must be seen the same hour, not the next day, because of that time window. Don’t wait to see if it eases.

Why: The safety-net is the single most important thing they leave with — for this presentation it must hammer the “sudden severe testicle pain = seen the same hour” message, since delay is what costs the testis. Say it plainly, every time.

What’s causing it

What’s actually causing all this?

Your symptoms fall into two patterns, and sorting which one is bothering you most tells us the cause. Trouble getting going — a weak or hesitant stream, straining, dribbling, never feeling quite empty — usually points to the outlet being narrowed, most often by an enlarged prostate. Trouble holding on — needing to go often, sudden urges, getting up at night — usually points to an overactive bladder. Many men have a bit of both, and we treat each part differently.

Honestly, you’re far from alone — a lot of men wait a long time before they say anything, and there’s nothing daft about it at all. It’s exactly the sort of thing worth raising, because most of it can be helped, and mentioning it means we can also rule out the few things that need catching early.

Why: Explicitly validate the embarrassment — many men delay for years. If he feels judged for raising it he won’t come back with the red-flag symptoms that matter.
Is it my prostate — is it cancer

Is it my prostate causing this?

Often, yes — in men your age a weak, hesitant stream is commonly the prostate gently squeezing the outlet as it enlarges. That enlargement itself is benign — not a cancer — but it’s worth us examining the prostate and talking through a blood test so we’re confident about what we’re dealing with.

Could this be prostate cancer?

It’s a very natural thing to worry about and I won’t brush it off. But because the two can sit side by side, we don’t just assume: we examine the prostate and can talk through a PSA blood test, so we’re actively checking rather than hoping.

Why: Never a flat “no” to the cancer question. Validate, explain LUTS ≠ cancer and that early cancer is often silent, but keep the DRE/PSA conversation honest — the workup is what reassures.

Should I have the PSA blood test?

It’s worth a proper conversation rather than a reflex tick-box. PSA can flag a prostate problem early, but it also rises with benign enlargement and infection, so a raised result doesn’t mean cancer and a normal one isn’t a full all-clear. In Ontario, OHIP covers it if it’s ordered because something’s suspected; as a routine screening test it’s usually around $40 out of pocket. Do you have to examine me — the back-passage exam?

It’s a quick examination of the prostate through the back passage, and it tells us a lot — the size, and whether it feels smooth and benign or has anything that needs a closer look. It’s over in moments and shouldn’t be painful, just a bit undignified. You can absolutely have a chaperone, and you can say no.

What can help

Can I just live with it — do I have to treat it?

If it’s not bothering you much and you’re emptying safely, then keeping an eye on it is a perfectly reasonable choice — plenty of men do exactly that. The main reason we’d nudge you toward treatment is if it’s wearing you down, or if the checks show your bladder isn’t emptying properly, because that can cause problems further down the line. So it’s your call, once we know it’s safe to leave.

Why: Honour “watchful waiting” as legitimate, but draw the line at incomplete emptying / high residual, which the source flags as the thing that changes management.

Is there anything I can do myself first?

Yes, and it’s often the sensible first step. Cutting back on fluids in the evening, easing off caffeine and alcohol, not drinking a lot right before bed, and treating any constipation can all make a real difference — sometimes enough that you don’t need tablets at all.

Will I need tablets or an operation?

Most men never need an operation. We usually start with the simple lifestyle changes, then tablets if those aren’t enough, and surgery is really reserved for when medicines don’t control things or there’s a complication like poor emptying. So think of it as a ladder we climb only as far as we need to.

What would the operation actually be — and when?

The usual operation for an enlarging prostate trims away the inner part that’s blocking the flow, done through the natural passage with a camera — no cut on the outside. We’d only reach for it when tablets aren’t keeping things comfortable, or if a complication turns up like repeated infections, poor emptying or an episode where you can’t pass urine at all. It’s not rushed: you’d see a specialist to weigh it up together, and the surgeon goes through exactly what it involves and takes your consent.

Why: Combines “what is surgery” with “when is it time” — the source names TURP but not its risks, so describe it plainly, tie the timing to the source’s complication triggers, and leave detailed risks and consent to the operating surgeon (PA scope).
The medications

How do the tablets work?

There are two main kinds for prostate-type symptoms. One — an alpha-blocker — relaxes the muscle around the outlet so urine flows more easily, and it tends to work within days. The other — a 5-alpha-reductase medicine — slowly shrinks a larger prostate over months. Sometimes we use one, sometimes both, depending on your prostate size. If the trouble is more an overactive bladder, we’d use a different tablet that calms the bladder instead.

Will these tablets affect my sex life?review

It’s a fair question and worth being straight about. The alpha-blocker can mean little or no fluid comes out when you climax — that’s reduced or absent ejaculate, not true backward flow into the bladder. It’s harmless and reverses if you stop. The prostate-shrinking medicine can, in some men, lower sex drive or affect erections, and rarely this lasts after stopping. Tell us about any of these, or any low mood, rather than just quietly stopping.

Why: Sexual side effects are the reason men abandon these drugs silently. Be honest and specific (anejaculation with alpha-blockers; libido/erectile effects with 5-ARI) but not alarmist, and invite them back rather than to stop unilaterally. Beyond the source — flag.

For most men this is a long-term thing rather than a short course, because the tablets manage the symptoms rather than cure the cause — stop them and the symptoms usually drift back. The prostate-shrinking one in particular needs a few months before you feel the benefit, so it’s worth sticking with it. If you ever want to come off, do it with us rather than suddenly.

What’s causing this

What’s actually causing this pain?

Honestly, in a lot of people there isn’t one single thing to point at — and that’s not us giving up, it’s the nature of this problem. What often happens is the nerves carrying pain signals from the pelvis get turned up too high, like a volume dial stuck loud, so the area keeps hurting even when the tissues underneath have settled. The muscles of the pelvic floor tightening up, the bladder, and the bowel can all feed into it too. So it’s usually several things overlapping rather than one lesion.

So there’s nothing actually wrong? Is this in my head?

No — the pain is completely real, and I want to be clear about that. When we say we can’t find a single lump or blockage, that isn’t the same as saying nothing’s wrong. The problem is real and it’s in the wiring — the nerves and muscles of the pelvis — not imagined. Pain that has no obvious cause on a scan is still pain, and it still deserves treating.

Why: The “it’s in your head” fear is the defining wound of this consultation — patients have often been dismissed before. Meet it head-on, name it as real, and separate “no single lesion found” from “nothing wrong.”

I’ve seen so many people — why can no one give me a straight answer?

I know how exhausting that is, and how it can feel like being passed around. The straight answer is that this is a condition where the tests are often normal precisely because the trouble is in how the nerves are behaving, not in something a scan can photograph. So the honest answer isn’t “there’s nothing there” — it’s “this is a recognised pain problem, and here’s the plan for it.” That’s a real answer, even if it’s not the one you were hoping for.

Is it something serious

Could this be cancer?

It’s a very natural thing to fear, and I won’t brush it off. This picture — long-standing pain with normal tests — is not what cancer usually looks like. But because I don’t want to just reassure you blindly, the checks we do (a urine test, a scan of the pelvis, and looking inside the bladder with a camera if the pain is bladder-centred) are partly there to actively rule cancer out. So we’re not ignoring the worry — we’re specifically looking, and that’s what lets us say it’s a pain problem rather than something sinister.

Why: Never a flat “no” to cancer. Validate, say why this pattern isn’t typical of cancer, then show the workup already hunts for it — honest reassurance earned by the tests, not asserted.

Isn’t it just an infection? Do I need antibiotics?

It’s a fair question, because an infection can cause pelvic pain and it must be ruled out first — so we test your urine. The thing is, in most people with this kind of long-term pain the urine is clear, with no bug growing. Occasionally, early on, one trial course is tried — but when there’s no infection, repeated courses don’t help the pain and can cause their own problems, so more courses aren’t the answer. That negative test is actually useful — it’s part of what tells us this is a pain condition rather than an infection we keep missing.

Why: Patients (and previous clinicians) often chase this with repeated antibiotics. Explain the sterile-urine finding as informative, and justify NOT prescribing without sounding dismissive of the symptom.

Is the pain damaging me, or getting worse in there?

This kind of pain, once we’ve ruled the serious causes out, isn’t a sign that something is being harmed or eaten away inside — the alarm is loud, but it isn’t doing damage. That matters, because fear that the pain equals harm can wind the whole thing tighter. It’s horrible to live with, but it isn’t destroying your insides.

How is it treated

If it’s not an infection, why not just try antibiotics anyway?

I understand the pull to just try something — but antibiotics work against infection, and beyond an occasional single early trial, more courses with no bug found don’t help this pain while still risking side effects and resistance. Reaching for them again tends to delay the treatment that actually helps. So instead of another course, I’d rather start you on the things that genuinely settle this kind of pain.

What actually helps, then?

One of the most useful things isn’t a tablet at all — it’s a special kind of physiotherapy for the pelvic floor. In a lot of people those muscles are clenched tight and tender, and learning to release and relax them can take a real edge off. Importantly it’s the opposite of pelvic-floor squeezes or Kegels, which usually make this kind of pain worse — so it’s about letting go, not tightening up.

You’re giving me an antidepressant / epilepsy tablet — but I’m not depressed and I don’t have fits?

You’re right to ask, because the leaflet will mention those uses. At low doses these medicines also quiet down over-sensitive nerves — that’s the whole reason we use them here, to turn that pain volume back down. It’s not that we think you’re depressed or having seizures; we’re borrowing the nerve-calming effect. If your mood or sleep are also suffering, they can help there too, which is a bonus rather than the reason.

Why: Amitriptyline/gabapentinoids/duloxetine feel like a hidden “it’s in your head” accusation. Pre-empting that — “we’re borrowing the nerve effect, not calling you depressed” — is essential to adherence.

Can I just have strong painkillers, like morphine?

I know when pain is this bad, the strongest thing sounds right — but for this particular kind of pain the heavy opioid painkillers genuinely don’t work well, and over time they cause real harm and can even make the pain system more sensitive. So it isn’t us withholding relief; it’s that they’re the wrong tool for this job. The nerve-calming medicines and the physio are what tend to move the needle here.

Why: Opioids are ineffective and harmful in CPPS but patients read refusal as disbelief. Frame as “wrong tool,” not “you don’t deserve relief.”

Why does something that helped my friend do nothing for me?

Because this isn’t one single condition — it’s a mix that’s put together slightly differently in each person. For one person the pelvic-floor muscles are the main driver; for another it’s the bladder, or the bowel, or the stress and poor sleep feeding it. So the treatment is matched to your particular pattern, which is why we tailor it rather than hand everyone the same tablet — and why some trial and error is normal, not a sign of failure.

Why are you sending me to so many different people?

Because no single person or pill treats all of this. A physiotherapist works on the muscles, a pain specialist on the medicines, and a psychologist on how the pain and stress feed each other — not because it’s “all in the mind,” but because managing the mind-body loop genuinely lowers the pain. Together that team does far more than any one of us on our own.

Why: A psychology referral can feel like the “it’s in your head” fear confirmed; naming that fear and reframing the referral as tackling a real mind-body loop keeps them onside.

Flares are part of the pattern and don’t mean you’ve gone backwards or done harm. Can I still work and drive?

For most people, yes — staying active and keeping some normal routine actually tends to help rather than harm, even though resting completely feels tempting. On a bad flare you may need to ease off, and if a job is very physical we can talk about adjustments. Low mood, anxiety and broken sleep aren’t a weakness, and they’re not the cause of your pain. But they do feed into it, so treating them — with talking therapy and, if it helps, medication — is a real part of getting on top of the pain, not a side issue.

What could be causing it

Could it just be from exercise or the gym?

It can, actually — a hard run or heavy exercise can put a bit of blood in the urine for a short while, and it settles. That’s one of the harmless causes we think about. But because it usually clears within a day or two, we’d confirm it’s gone by repeating the test rather than just assuming that’s all it was.

Why: Named benign transient cause in the workup (recent vigorous exercise). Validate it, then explain the safety-catch (re-test to confirm it clears) so “it was just the gym” isn’t used to skip a genuinely needed check.

Could it be my period — could that have thrown it off?

Very possibly. Menstrual blood can find its way into the sample and read as blood in the urine when it isn’t really coming from the urinary tract at all. The simple fix is to repeat the test away from your period on a clean sample, so we know what we’re really dealing with.

Why: Menstruation is a listed transient cause. Keep it matter-of-fact and give the concrete workaround (re-test off-period) rather than dismissing — a genuine finding could otherwise be missed.

Could it just be a bladder infection?

It can — an infection irritates the bladder and can leave a little blood behind. If there are signs of one, we treat it first and then re-check the urine once it’s cleared. If the blood’s gone, great; if it’s still there afterwards, that tells us to keep looking rather than putting it down to the infection.

Why: Cystitis is in the differential; the workup says exclude and re-test once treated. The teaching point is the re-check after treatment — persistent blood after a treated infection is what pushes on with the workup.

Could it be my blood-thinner or a tablet I’m on?

People often assume a blood-thinner explains it, but here’s the honest bit: being on one doesn’t give us a free pass to stop looking. It can be there at the same time as a real cause, so we’d still check things out the same way rather than blaming the tablet and closing the book.

Why: review: presentation entry does not list anticoagulants as a cause — kept deliberately generic and standard (anticoagulation is NOT an adequate explanation for haematuria; still investigate). Clinician to confirm local wording/threshold.

I’m an older man — could it just be my prostate?

It can be — an enlarged prostate is a common, benign reason for a little blood in the urine as men get older, and it may well turn out to be that. But we can’t safely assume it without checking, because a couple of the causes we screen for get a bit more likely with age too. So we take the prostate seriously as a likely answer and still do the checks.

Why: BPH is in the differential as benign prostatic bleeding in older men. Offer it as a plausible benign cause while being honest that age also raises the urothelial-cancer risk factor — so it doesn’t become a reason to skip the workup.
Day to day

Do I need to change anything or stop doing things while I wait?

No — you can carry on completely as normal. There’s nothing you’re doing that’s making it worse, and nothing you need to stop. Keep drinking normally, work, exercise, all fine. This is about checking, not about you being unwell.

Why: The patient feels well; over-restricting daily life would manufacture the illness-anxiety the brief warns against. Explicitly free them to carry on.

Is it dangerous to leave it a few weeks until the tests?

A short wait for the tests is safe — this isn’t an emergency, and these causes don’t change overnight. The one thing that would change the plan is if you actually see red or pink urine; that moves you into a faster track, so let us know straight away if that happens. Otherwise, waiting the usual few weeks is perfectly fine.

Why: Reassure on the wait (avoids catastrophising) while planting the visible-haematuria safety-net from whatToWatch — any VISIBLE blood skips the risk-grading and gets the urgent pathway.
When the tests are clear — what next?

If all the tests come back clear, is that the end of it?

For a lot of people, yes — clear tests are genuinely good news and often the finding just fades. What we usually do instead of scanning you all over again is simply re-check the urine at a follow-up. If it’s settled, we can draw a line under it; if it keeps showing, we’d have another think rather than ignore it.

Why: Directly answers the brief’s “what if clear — do I need repeat checks”. The management field is explicit: negative workup → follow up with repeat urinalysis rather than re-imaging. Frame the repeat test as light-touch, not a red flag.

What if you never find out what caused it?

That’s actually a common and reassuring ending — for many people no cause is ever found, and once the serious ones are ruled out, no answer is a good answer here. It means we’ve looked in the right places and not found anything that needs treating. You don’t need a named culprit to be safe.

Why: “No cause found” is the modal outcome and the management plan accepts it. Reframe the absence of a diagnosis as reassuring rather than as unfinished business, so the patient isn’t left anxious about a missing answer.

When should I come back sooner rather than wait?

Come back sooner if you actually see blood — red or pink urine — because that gets looked at faster than this does. Also let us know if you get burning or pain passing urine that doesn’t settle, a fever, or pain in your side or back. Short of those, waiting for your booked tests is exactly right.

Why: Every set ends on the safety-net (mirrors renal_colic red_flags). The headline is visible haematuria (whatToWatch) → faster track; the rest are plain, general red flags kept safely generic.

When patients ask…
Why do I keep getting these?

Why do I keep getting these — what’s wrong with me?

For most women who get repeated bladder infections, honestly there’s nothing “wrong” in the sense of a disease — it’s that the bacteria that live around us have a short trip to the bladder, and some people are simply more prone to reinfection. It doesn’t mean you’re dirty or doing something wrong, and it doesn’t mean it’ll be like this forever.

Why: Source frames this as usually reinfection in otherwise-healthy women. Lead by defusing shame — a patient who feels blamed disengages from prevention.

Is it the same infection coming back, or a new one each time?

Usually it’s a brand-new infection rather than the old one never clearing — which is actually the more reassuring version. We can tell the two apart by sending a urine sample off to be grown each time, and that’s partly why we ask you to get a sample in before starting antibiotics.

Why: Cultures distinguish reinfection from relapse (source workup). Reinfection is the common, lower-risk pattern; relapse hints at an enabling nidus.

How many does it take before it counts as “recurrent”?

Roughly two confirmed infections in six months, or three in a year. That’s not a magic number — it’s just the point where it’s worth stepping back from treating each one and instead making a proper plan to stop them.

Could there be an underlying reason nobody’s found?

In an otherwise-healthy woman, usually not — it’s just proneness. But in a man, in a child, or when the bacteria are unusual, we do look harder, because then a repeat infection can be a sign of something to fix underneath — like a stone, a bladder that doesn’t empty fully, or in older men the prostate. If you’re in one of those groups we’ll investigate rather than just keep prescribing.

Why: Source red flags: recurrent UTI in a man/child/atypical organism demands a structural or functional explanation. Be honest that the workup is risk-stratified.

One result said an unusual bug — does that matter?

It can, so it’s worth mentioning. Certain bacteria go hand in hand with stones in the urinary tract, so if one of those keeps showing up we’d want a scan to check. It doesn’t mean anything’s definitely wrong — it just lowers our threshold to look.

Why: Source: urease-producing organisms (Proteus) → suspect a stone. Kept generic, no organism named to the patient.
Will it damage my kidneys?

Will all these infections damage my kidneys?

For the common type — infections that stay down in the bladder — the honest answer is that they’re miserable but they don’t tend to scar the kidneys. The kind we do worry about is when an infection climbs up to the kidney itself, which makes you properly unwell with a fever and back pain. That’s exactly the one we want you to come straight back for, so we can treat it fast.

Why: Source separates recurrent lower-tract (usual) from recurrent pyelonephritis (image and protect kidneys). Reassure on cystitis, flag the upper-tract escalation.

Do I need a scan or a camera test?

Not everyone does. For a healthy woman with straightforward bladder infections, a scan often doesn’t change anything. We tend to arrange one — usually an ultrasound, sometimes a bladder scan to check you’re emptying properly, and occasionally a camera look inside the bladder — if you’re a man, if the bacteria are unusual, if there’s a hint of a stone or a blockage, or if there’s blood in your urine between infections.

Why: Source workup: post-void residual, US ± CT, cystoscopy reserved for men/atypical organisms/suspected stone/obstruction. Shared imaging group covers scan logistics.

They mentioned checking if my bladder empties — why?

If a bit of urine is always left behind after you go, that stale pool is a comfortable place for bacteria to breed, and it can drive infection after infection. It’s a quick, painless scan of your bladder just after you’ve been to the washroom, and if it shows a problem, sorting the emptying can matter more than any antibiotic.

Why: Source: post-void residual — incomplete emptying drives recurrence; management stresses treating the enabling cause, not just each episode.

I sometimes see blood — is that just the infection?

Blood during an infection is common and usually settles once it clears. What we don’t want to wave away is blood that keeps showing up between infections, when you feel well — that we always check out properly, just to be safe. So do tell us if you’re seeing it at times when you’ve no other symptoms.

Why: Source red flag: persistent haematuria between infections → exclude malignancy. Validate the common case, ring-fence the between-episodes pattern.
Preventing them — what you can do

Everyone says “drink more water” — is that really all there is?

I know how that lands when you’ve heard it a hundred times. Fluids genuinely do help by flushing the bladder, so it’s not nothing — but you’re right that it isn’t the whole story. There’s a proper ladder of things we can try beyond it, from simple habits up to medical options, and we work through them together rather than leaving you with just “drink more”.

Why: Directly answers the brief’s frustration. Validate, keep hydration honest as one rung, then signpost the ladder (source management).

Are there habits that actually make a difference?review

A few simple ones are worth doing because they’re harmless and may help — not holding on when you need to go, and emptying your bladder rather than rushing. I’ll be straight with you that the evidence for a lot of the classic hygiene “rules” is weaker than people assume, so I won’t load you with a long list of things to feel guilty about.

Why: Source lists voiding habits under behavioural prevention. Be honest that much hygiene advice is folklore — avoid the guilt-list the brief warns against.

Does cranberry actually work?

It can help a bit. Good-quality studies suggest cranberry products cut the number of infections by roughly a quarter in women who get repeat infections. It’s modest, not a guarantee, and not a substitute for the stronger options if you’re getting a lot of them. If it’s not upsetting your stomach or clashing with other medicines, it’s a reasonable thing to try.

Why: Updated to Cochrane 2023 (CD001321): cranberry probably reduces symptomatic culture-verified UTI in women with recurrent UTI (RR 0.74); no benefit shown in older adults, bladder-emptying problems or pregnancy.

Is there a preventive that isn’t an antibiotic?

Yes — there’s a tablet that makes your urine a hostile place for bacteria to grow, without being an antibiotic, so it doesn’t breed resistance the way antibiotics can. We often try that sort of non-antibiotic prevention before committing you to long-term antibiotics.

Why: Source management: methenamine hippurate as non-antibiotic prophylaxis, tried before antibiotics. Named generically by mechanism, no brand.

I’m past the menopause — is there anything specific for me?

There can be. After the menopause the tissues around the bladder opening get thinner and drier, which makes infections easier, and a low-dose oestrogen you use vaginally — as a cream or pessary — can genuinely cut the number you get. It works locally, so it’s a different thing from taking hormones as tablets, and it’s worth asking about if this is you.

Why: Source: topical vaginal oestrogen in post-menopausal women; workup assesses for atrophic vaginitis. Distinguish local from systemic HRT to pre-empt the worry.
Antibiotics — resistance, standby, long-term

Can I just keep antibiotics at home for when it starts?

For the right person, yes — it’s a real option we call self-start treatment: you keep a course and begin it as soon as you feel the tell-tale symptoms, often after popping a sample in. It suits people who know their symptoms well and don’t get them too often. It isn’t for everyone, because starting antibiotics on a guess every time can breed resistance and mask something else — so we’d agree together whether it fits you.

Why: Source management: patient-initiated self-start therapy is a listed option. Offer it genuinely, but pair with stewardship caveat per the brief.

Could I take a small daily antibiotic to stop them?

That’s an option too — a low dose taken regularly for a stretch of months to keep them at bay. It works well for a lot of people. We usually reach for it after we’ve tried the simpler and non-antibiotic steps, and we review it rather than leaving you on it indefinitely, because the more antibiotic we use, the more we push resistance along.

Why: Source: low-dose continuous prophylaxis, placed after behavioural/non-antibiotic rungs on the ladder.

Will I be on antibiotics forever?

That’s not the plan, and I understand why you’d dread it. When we do use a preventive antibiotic it’s usually for a defined stretch, then we stop and see how you get on — often things are quieter afterwards. The aim is always to get you off them, not to keep you on them, which is why we bother with all the other steps first.

Why: Directly addresses the brief’s “will I be on antibiotics forever” worry. Frame as time-limited and reviewed, not open-ended.

I keep hearing about resistance — is that why you won’t just prescribe?

Partly, yes, and it’s not us being stingy. Every time bacteria meet an antibiotic they don’t need, the survivors get a bit tougher — so the day you truly need one, it’s more likely to work if we’ve used them carefully all along. So when I hold back, I’m protecting your future infections, not dismissing this one.

Why: Stewardship rationale, reframed as protecting the patient rather than rationing. The brief flags resistance worry explicitly.

The antibiotics clear it, so why does it keep coming back?

Because an antibiotic only mops up the infection that’s there — it doesn’t change whatever’s letting them keep starting. If there’s a fixable reason underneath, like a stone or a bladder that doesn’t empty, treating that is what actually breaks the cycle. Where there’s no such cause, it’s about the prevention steps rather than the antibiotic itself.

Why: Central source message: treat the enabling lesion (stone/residual/prostate), not just each episode. Antibiotics clear episodes but don’t fix the reason.

Is this linked to sex?

For a lot of women, yes — infections that reliably turn up a day or so after sex are a well-recognised pattern, and it’s nothing to be embarrassed about. It’s simply that sex can nudge bacteria towards the bladder. The good news is that pattern is one of the easier ones to help.

Am I causing this with poor hygiene?

Almost certainly not, and I want to take that worry off you. Recurrent infections aren’t a sign of being unclean — over-washing or harsh products can actually irritate things and make matters worse. Gentle and normal is exactly right.

When should I worry — when do I come straight back?

Also come back if you can’t pass urine at all, if you keep seeing blood, or if it simply isn’t settling on treatment.

Kidney stone prevention

When patients ask…
Why do I keep forming stones?

Why did I form a stone in the first place?

A stone forms when your urine gets over-concentrated with the salts that build stones, so they crystallise out instead of staying dissolved — a bit like sugar coming out of an over-sweet cup of tea. Usually it’s a mix of not quite enough fluid and some tendency in how your body handles those salts. The tests we do are exactly to work out which of those is driving yours.

Is this my diet or is it in my genes?

Honestly, it’s usually a bit of both, and the balance is different for everyone. Some people have an inherited tendency that no amount of careful eating fully cancels out; for others, small habits — mainly how much they drink and how much salt they eat — make most of the difference. That’s the whole point of testing rather than guessing: so any advice we give you is actually aimed at your cause.

Why: Patients often arrive either blaming themselves or expecting one magic culprit. Name both, refuse the single-cause story, and pivot to “that’s why we test” so the workup feels purposeful.

Did I do something wrong to cause this?

No — and please don’t carry this as a blame thing. Plenty of people who eat carefully still form stones, and plenty who don’t never do. It’s much more about the chemistry of your urine than about willpower. What we can do is find the levers that actually work for you and pull those.

Why: A blamed patient disengages from the very prevention advice this clinic exists to give. Mirrors the renal_colic “my_fault” defuse, kept distinct from it.

Does it matter what my stone was made of?

It matters more than almost anything else. Most stones are calcium-based, but some are made of uric acid, some come from infection, and a rare few are inherited — and the prevention for each is genuinely different. If you managed to catch and hand in the stone, analysing it is one of the single most useful things we have.

Why: The stone analysis drives the whole plan. Flag early that advice is type-specific so later “is this right for me?” answers land.

What if I lost the stone — can you still help?

Yes. Analysing a passed stone is ideal, but if it’s gone, the urine collection and bloods still tell us a great deal about which way your chemistry leans. If you ever pass another, do try to strain your urine and catch it — even a tiny fragment is genuinely useful for pinning down the type.

The tests to find out why

What tests will you actually do?

Two main things beyond looking at the stone itself. One is a set of blood tests — to check your calcium, a gland hormone that controls calcium, your urate level and a couple of salts. The other is a 24-hour urine collection, where you save all your urine for a full day so we can measure exactly what’s in it. Between them they usually point to the abnormality we can target.

Why do I have to collect my urine for a whole day?

Because a one-off sample is just a snapshot, and it changes hour to hour with what you’ve eaten and drunk. Collecting everything over a full 24 hours gives us the true daily totals — how much fluid you’re really passing, and how much calcium, oxalate, citrate, uric acid and salt come out. That’s the test that tells us which lever to pull, so it’s worth doing it properly.

Why: The 24-h collection is the cornerstone of the workup but feels tedious. Justify why a spot sample won’t do, so they complete it accurately.

What are the blood tests looking for?

Mainly a few treatable causes hiding in the background. We check your calcium and a gland hormone that controls it, because an overactive gland can quietly push calcium into your urine and make stones. We also look at your urate and your body’s acid balance, since those point to other stone types. If one of those turns up, it changes the plan.

Why: Ties the bloods to specific mechanisms (hyperparathyroidism, urate, tubular acidosis) without jargon, so the patient sees the test has a purpose, not a fishing trip.
Diet — what actually helps

Should I cut out calcium and dairy?

This is the big myth, and I’m glad you asked — for most people the answer is no. It sounds logical because most stones contain calcium, but cutting dietary calcium usually backfires: with less calcium in your gut, more of a substance called oxalate gets absorbed and ends up in your urine, and that can actually make stones more likely. A normal amount of calcium in your food is what we want. Where calcium can be a problem is in supplement pills — mention those to us.

Why: The single most important myth to bust in this clinic. Explain the mechanism (low dietary calcium → higher oxalate absorption) plainly, and separate food-calcium from supplements — the honest nuance.

How much should I drink?

This is the biggest single lever you have. Aim to drink enough that you’re passing well over two litres of urine a day — in practice that usually means drinking somewhere around two-and-a-half to three litres, more in hot weather or if you sweat a lot. The simplest guide is your urine colour: keep it pale, like pale straw, rather than dark. Spread it through the day, and have a glass before bed.

Why: Fluids prevent most recurrences — the highest-yield advice. Give a concrete output target and the pale-urine rule of thumb, since “drink more” alone is too vague to act on.

Does salt really matter that much?

More than most people expect. A high-salt diet makes your kidneys spill more calcium into the urine, which feeds calcium stones. Cutting back on obviously salty foods and not adding much at the table is a genuinely useful change — and it’s good for your blood pressure into the bargain. A lot of the salt is hidden in processed and takeaway food rather than the salt-pot.

Why: Salt–calcium link is under-appreciated by patients. Point at hidden/processed salt so the advice is actionable, not just “eat less salt”.

I’ve read I should avoid oxalate — is that right?

Only for some people. Oxalate is in things like spinach, rhubarb, nuts, beetroot and a lot of tea and chocolate, and if your urine collection shows your oxalate is high, cutting down on the biggest sources helps. But if your oxalate is normal, stripping these healthy foods out gains you little — so this is one to steer by your test rather than blanket-avoid. And remember the calcium point: keeping normal calcium in the same meal actually mops up some oxalate before it’s absorbed.

Why: Honest “it depends on your result” answer. Oxalate restriction is test-driven, not universal; tie it back to the calcium myth to reinforce the mechanism.

Do I need to give up meat?

You don’t have to give it up, but easing back on animal protein — red meat, but also a lot of fish and poultry — helps if you’re a stone-former. A heavy meat intake makes your urine more acidic and raises the salts that build several stone types. Think moderating portions rather than going fully vegetarian, unless we’ve found a specific reason for stricter advice.

Why: Protein/purine load feeds calcium and uric-acid stones. Frame as moderation to keep it realistic and avoid an all-or-nothing reaction.

Is there one diet that stops all stones?

There isn’t, and anyone selling you one is overselling it. The universal bits are true for nearly everyone — drink plenty, go easy on salt and heavy meat, keep a normal calcium intake. Beyond that, the right tweaks depend on your stone type and what your urine test shows. That’s deliberately not one-size-fits-all, and it’s why we’d rather tailor it than hand you a generic leaflet.

Why: Sets honest expectations and gives agency: universal basics + a personalised layer. Counters the “just tell me the diet” demand without dismissing it.
Do I need medication?

Will I need tablets to prevent them?

Not everyone does. For a lot of people, getting the fluids and diet right is enough. We tend to add a medicine when your urine test shows a specific abnormality that a tablet can correct, or when stones keep coming despite doing the lifestyle side well. So it’s a considered add-on, not an automatic one.

Why: Patients either fear lifelong pills or want a quick fix. Position medication as targeted and test-driven, protecting the “fluids first” message.

What sort of tablets are they?

It depends entirely on what we find. For some calcium stone-formers we use a water tablet that, counter-intuitively, makes the kidney hold on to calcium instead of dumping it into the urine. For others we use something that raises a natural stone-inhibitor in the urine and gently de-acidifies it. For uric-acid stones, de-acidifying the urine is the main move and can even help dissolve them. I’ll only go into the one that fits your result.

Why: Illustrates that the drug follows the mechanism (thiazide, citrate, alkalinisation) in lay terms, without brand names or over-detailing meds the patient may never take.

What are the side effects of the prevention tablets?

That depends which one we settle on, so I’ll go through the specific ones when we know. As a rule these are long-term, low-dose medicines that most people tolerate well, and we usually check a blood test or two after starting to make sure your salts stay balanced. I’ll always tell you the handful of things that mean call us rather than wait.

Why: Overrides the generic SHARED_ASK meds framing with the stone-prevention specifics: long-term, monitored with bloods. Keep drug-specific detail deferred until the agent is chosen.

Will I be on them for life?

Often these are long-term medicines, because they work by keeping your urine chemistry favourable — stop them and the tendency can drift back. But it’s not set in stone: if follow-up tests look good and your stones stay away, we can review whether you still need them. It’s a conversation we keep having, not a life sentence handed down on day one.

Monitoring & follow-up

How will we know if any of this is working?

A couple of ways. We usually repeat the 24-hour urine collection a few months after you’ve made the changes or started a tablet, to check the numbers we were targeting have actually shifted in the right direction. And we keep an eye on your stone situation with imaging over time. If the chemistry’s improved but you didn’t “feel” anything change, that’s fine — this is a numbers game as much as a symptoms one.

Why: Prevention has no daily symptom to track, which frustrates patients. Explain the repeat 24-h and imaging so “working” is defined by objective markers, not how they feel.

Will I keep having scans?

Usually some, spaced out, so we can see whether you’re staying clear or quietly building new stones — the ones inside the kidney often cause no symptoms until they move. How often depends on your risk; we don’t scan for the sake of it, but we also don’t like being caught out. I’ll tell you the plan for you.

How long will you keep seeing me for this?

That depends on how things go. If you’re a one-off stone with reassuring tests, follow-up may be fairly short. If you’re forming them repeatedly, or you’ve got a type that needs watching, we tend to stay in touch longer-term. The aim is to hand you the tools and step back when it’s safe to, not to keep you tethered.

Will I get another — and when to come straight back

Will I definitely get another stone?

Not definitely — but honestly, once you’ve formed one there’s a real chance of another over the years if nothing changes. I won’t pretend that away. The good news is that this is one of the more preventable things we deal with: getting the fluids right alone shifts the odds a lot, and the tailored steps shift them further. So the number isn’t fixed — a lot of it is now in your hands.

Why: Do not falsely reassure — recurrence is common. Give the honest risk, then immediately hand back agency via prevention, mirroring the renal_colic “come_back” arc without duplicating its wording.

If I do everything right, am I guaranteed to be stone-free?

No guarantee, I’m afraid — some people still form the odd stone despite doing everything well, especially if there’s a strong inherited streak. But “doing everything right” genuinely reduces both how often they come and, often, how much trouble they cause when they do. Think of it as heavily stacking the odds in your favour rather than a cast-iron promise.

Why: Prevents the patient feeling cheated by a later recurrence. Honest ceiling on what lifestyle can achieve, while keeping the effort worthwhile.

Should my children or family get checked?

There can be a family tendency, but there’s no routine screening we’d put your relatives through just because you’ve had a stone. Where it’s worth a look is if several close relatives are affected, or if yours turns out to be one of the inherited types — I’d flag that specifically. Otherwise, the same sensible habits, mainly drinking well, are good advice for anyone.

When should I come straight back?

Come straight back — as an emergency — if you get the tell-tale severe waves of loin-to-groin pain of a new stone on the move, and especially if that pain comes with a fever or the shivers, if you can’t keep fluids down, or if you can’t pass urine at all. A fever alongside stone pain needs seeing urgently, because a blocked, infected kidney turns serious fast. Don’t sit on that one.

Why: The safety-net for a NEW acute episode is the most important thing they leave with. Restated here deliberately (not just left to renal_colic) because this clinic patient may next present acutely; fever + obstruction is the emergency.

When patients ask…
What a raised PSA actually means

What is PSA — what does a high number mean?

PSA is a protein your prostate makes that shows up in a blood test. A raised level tells us the prostate is worth a closer look — it doesn’t tell us why it’s up. Think of it as a flag to look further, not a diagnosis.

Why: Set the frame early — “prostate-specific, not cancer-specific.” Everything that follows hangs off this one line.

How high is mine — is that a lot?

There’s no single cut-off that means trouble on its own — it’s read alongside your age, your prostate size, and how it changes over time. The trend across tests often tells us more than any single number, which is why we usually repeat it before doing anything.

Does this mean I have cancer

Does this mean I’ve got cancer?

A raised PSA is a flag to look further, not a diagnosis — so the honest answer is: we don’t know yet, and that’s exactly why we test more. Many raised PSAs are not cancer; they come from a bigger prostate, inflammation, or even something as ordinary as a recent ejaculation. But because it can be an early sign, we don’t ignore it — we look properly.

Why: The core calibrated-honesty item. Never a flat “no” and never a flat “yes”: name that many raised PSAs aren’t cancer, without falsely clearing them. Mirrors the renal_colic cancer answer.

I feel completely well — can it still be serious?

Yes, it can — early prostate problems often cause no symptoms at all, which is exactly why a blood test can pick something up before you’d ever feel it. Feeling well is reassuring, but it isn’t the same as an all-clear, so it’s still worth finishing the checks.

What raises PSA apart from cancer

What else can push the PSA up?

Quite a few everyday things. A prostate that’s simply enlarged with age raises it. So does inflammation or infection of the prostate, a recent bladder infection, a catheter, or an examination of the prostate done just before the blood was taken. That’s why the story around the test matters as much as the number.

I had sex / went cycling before the test — does that count?

Recent ejaculation can nudge the PSA up a little for a day or two, so we’ll ask you to avoid that for a couple of days before the repeat. Cycling doesn’t seem to make a real difference in studies. Either way, it’s one reason we don’t act on a single reading.

Why: Naming the ejaculation raiser plainly (a 2015 meta-analysis found no significant cycling effect) defuses a lot of anxiety and sets up why the repeat test is done under fairer conditions.

Is this something I did?

No — a raised PSA isn’t about anything you did wrong. A prostate tends to grow and get a bit more active as men get older, and that’s the commonest reason it’s up. This isn’t about blame; it’s just something we’ve spotted and are checking.

Why: Defuse guilt, as in the exemplar — a blamed patient disengages. The commonest cause (benign enlargement) is genuinely nobody’s fault.
The next tests — repeat PSA, examination and MRI

Why repeat the blood test — can’t you tell now?

Because a single reading can be thrown off by everyday things like a recent infection or ejaculation. Repeating it after a few weeks tells us whether the rise is real and lasting or just a blip. Since we’re following up a raised result, OHIP normally covers it — it’s routine screening tests that usually cost around $40.

Do you need to examine me down there?

Usually yes — a quick examination of the prostate with a gloved finger (a “DRE”) tells us its size and whether it feels smooth or has a firm area. It takes seconds and, together with the blood test, helps us decide what’s next. Where we can, we take the blood first, so there’s no question about the reading.

What’s the MRI for?

If the PSA stays up or the examination raises a question, we scan the prostate with a detailed MRI. It maps the prostate and shows whether there’s any area that looks suspicious and worth sampling. It’s a picture-only test — no needles, nothing goes inside for the scan itself.

If the MRI’s clear, can I avoid a biopsy?

Often, yes — and that’s exactly why we scan before we ever pick up a needle. A reassuring MRI can mean no biopsy is needed at all, and where a biopsy is still wanted it lets us aim straight at the area of concern rather than sampling blindly. Scanning first spares a lot of men a biopsy they’d otherwise have had.

Why: This is the MRI-before-biopsy pathway — the single most reassuring true thing to say. Frame the MRI as the gatekeeper that reduces unnecessary biopsies.
The biopsy — what it involves and its risks

Do I definitely need a biopsy?

Not necessarily. Whether you need one depends on how the repeat blood test, the examination and the MRI come together. Plenty of men get this far and don’t need a biopsy at all. If it is recommended, someone will explain why in your case and go through it properly with you.

Why: Keep the biopsy conditional, not inevitable — over-stating its likelihood raises anxiety the whole pathway is trying to lower.

What actually happens in a biopsy?

A few tiny samples are taken from the prostate with a fine needle, guided so they’re aimed at any area the scan flagged. It’s usually quick, and it’s done with numbing or, in some cases, while you’re asleep — whoever’s doing it will tell you which applies to you.

Will the biopsy hurt?

You’ll feel some pressure and a few brief pinches, but it’s made as comfortable as possible with numbing, and some men have it done asleep. It’s uncomfortable more than it is painful for most, and it’s over fairly quickly. Afterwards you can expect some soreness for a short while.

Is the biopsy safe — what could go wrong?

It’s generally safe and done very commonly, but I won’t pretend it’s risk-free. The main things to know are a chance of infection — which is why you’re often given antibiotics around it — and some bleeding, so it’s normal to see a little blood in your urine, your semen, or from the back passage for a while afterwards. Serious problems are uncommon. The person doing it will go through all of this and take your consent beforehand.

Why: Honest about the two real risks — infection and bleeding — structured minor→uncommon, then note antibiotics and that blood in urine/semen is expected not alarming. Consent is the doer’s job, per scope.

You said I might see blood — for how long?

A little blood in your urine or semen after a prostate biopsy is expected and can linger for a few weeks, especially in the semen — that’s normal and settles on its own. What isn’t normal is heavy bleeding, fever or shivers, or trouble passing urine, and those mean you should get seen.

Why: Pre-warn so expected bleeding doesn’t frighten them into thinking it failed — and pin the come-back triggers (fever, heavy bleeding, retention) to it.
If it is cancer — and if we watch it instead

And if it does turn out to be cancer?

Then we’d talk it through properly, because prostate cancer isn’t one single thing — some are slow and may never cause you trouble, others need treating. If it comes to that, we grade it and stage it so any advice you get fits your actual situation, rather than a worst-case guess. That’s a conversation we’d have with the full picture in front of us, not now.

Why: Keep this brief and honest per the brief — signpost that prostate cancer is a spectrum and that specifics wait for real information, without minimising.

Can’t we just watch it instead of doing anything?

Sometimes that’s genuinely the right plan — for a low-risk cancer, keeping a close eye on it (called active surveillance) with regular tests can be safer than rushing into treatment you might not need. But “watch it” is a proper monitored plan we’d choose together once we know what we’re dealing with, not the same as ignoring it.

Why: Honour the watch-and-wait instinct — active surveillance is real and often right — but draw the line: it’s monitored, not neglect. Mirrors the exemplar “do nothing” item.
When to come back

Is there anything that means I should come back sooner?

Yes — come back if you get new bone pain that won’t settle, blood in your urine, or trouble passing urine, and don’t wait for your next appointment for those. If you’ve just had a biopsy, a fever or the shivers, heavy bleeding, or being unable to pass urine need to be seen straight away.

Why: The safety-net is what they leave with. Combines the presentation red flags (new bone pain, haematuria) with the post-biopsy emergencies (infection, heavy bleeding, retention). Say it plainly, every time.

The waiting between tests is the hard part — is that safe?

It really is the hard part, and I won’t pretend otherwise. The good news is that the few weeks we wait to repeat the test or arrange a scan don’t change the outcome — prostate changes are usually slow. If anything new comes up in the meantime, like the symptoms we just talked about, that’s your cue to call us rather than sit on it.

Tests & scansshared

Does the scan hurt?

No — it’s painless. An ultrasound is a bit of cool gel and a probe pressed gently on your skin. A CT is a few minutes lying still while the table slides through a ring. An MRI means lying still in a tunnel-like scanner for about half an hour or more, and it’s noisy, so you get ear protection. We’ll tell you which one you’re having.

Is the radiation dangerous?

Ultrasound and MRI use no radiation at all. A CT scan does, but the dose is small, and we only do one when what it tells us is worth it. For the odd scan the risk is tiny next to the benefit of getting the diagnosis right.

Why: Answer the proportionality honestly rather than “it’s totally safe” — patients trust a real risk-benefit more than a brush-off.

The dye — is that safe, especially for my kidneys?

For most people it’s very safe. We check a kidney blood test first, and you flush it out afterwards with normal drinking. Do tell us about any past reaction to contrast or bad allergies.

Why: Name the two real caveats — renal function and prior reaction — so it reads as careful, not dismissive.

When will I get the results?

Some results we can tell you on the day, but a full scan report usually takes a few days. Either way, someone will talk you through what it shows before any decisions are made — you won’t be left guessing.

The procedure & anaestheticshared

Will I be awake or asleep?

It depends on the procedure — many are done under a general anaesthetic so you feel and remember nothing, and some smaller ones under a local. Whoever’s doing it will tell you which, and why.

How long will I be in hospital?

A lot of these are day cases — in and home the same day. Bigger operations mean a night or a few. We’ll give you a realistic idea for your one so you can plan.

Can I eat and drink beforehand?

If you’re having an anaesthetic you’ll be asked not to eat for a few hours before — we’ll give you exact timings. Miss those and the procedure can get delayed, so they matter.

Can I drive myself home afterwards?

Not after sedation or a general anaesthetic — you’ll need someone to take you home and ideally stay with you for the first day. Best to sort that out in advance.

What the test showed

What did the test actually find?

The urine sample grew some bacteria. That sounds alarming, but on its own it just means a few bugs are living in the urine — which is surprisingly common, especially as we get older or if there’s a catheter. It only counts as an infection when it’s making you unwell, and right now it isn’t.

So I’ve got a urine infection?

Not in the way that word usually means. A true urine infection comes with symptoms — stinging, needing to go all the time, pain, feeling feverish. What you’ve got is bacteria in the urine with none of that, and we treat those two things very differently.

Why: The whole consultation turns on separating “bacteria present” from “infection” — draw that line early and everything after it makes sense.

Then why was my urine tested at all?

Often it gets checked as part of looking into something else, and the bacteria turn up by chance. That’s a good moment to say: we try not to test urine when there aren’t symptoms, precisely because a result like this can send people down a treatment path they never needed.

Why: Names the overtesting problem gently — the incidental positive is the origin of most inappropriate treatment.
Why we’re not giving antibiotics

Why aren’t you giving me antibiotics?

Because here they’d do more harm than good. With no symptoms, antibiotics won’t make you feel any better — there’s nothing to feel better from — but they still carry side-effects and they help bugs become resistant, so the drug won’t work when you genuinely need it one day. Holding off is the active, careful choice, not us cutting corners.

Why: This is the counter-intuitive teaching point. Frame non-treatment as a positive decision made FOR the patient, not a refusal — patients hear “no antibiotics” as “no care” unless you reframe it.

It feels like you’re not treating me — is that safe?

I understand it can feel that way, and it’s a fair thing to ask. But treating this wouldn’t be doing more for you — it’d be doing something to you that only has downsides. Safe care here is watching sensibly and knowing exactly when to step in, which we’ll set up together.

Why: Directly addresses the “withholding” fear that undermines the plan. The reassurance is that a plan + safety-net IS the treatment.

What’s the harm in just taking them to be safe?

Two real harms. Antibiotics upset a lot of people — nausea, yeast infections, sometimes a nasty gut infection called C. difficile. And every unnecessary course nudges bacteria towards resistance, so the next infection is harder to treat. “Just in case” isn’t free.

Won’t it spread or get worse?

Won’t it spread up to my kidneys?

That’s the sensible worry, and the reassuring answer is that bacteria sitting quietly in the urine like this very rarely climb up and cause a kidney infection. Treating them with antibiotics doesn’t actually lower that small chance — which is another reason it’s not worth the downsides.

Why: The kidney-spread fear is often what drives the demand for antibiotics; answer it head-on AND note that treatment doesn’t reduce the risk, which is the evidence that removes the rationale.

Will it get worse if we leave it?

For most people it just sits there and often clears by itself. It doesn’t “build up” into something dangerous while we watch. What we’re looking out for isn’t it getting worse silently — it’s you noticing new symptoms, which is the signal that changes the plan.

But my urine smells and looks cloudy

My urine is cloudy and smells — surely that’s an infection?

It really feels like it should be, but cloudy or strong-smelling urine on its own isn’t a reason for antibiotics. It’s very often just how concentrated the urine is — a bit of dehydration will do it — and it can look and smell like that with no infection at all. Symptoms are what count, not the look or the smell.

Why: A specific, common, high-yield misconception. Patients (and families) treat cloudy/smelly urine as proof — say plainly it is not an indication, and offer the innocent explanation.

Would drinking more sort the smell out?

Very often, yes. When urine is more dilute it’s usually paler and less strong-smelling, so keeping well hydrated is a fair thing to try and it does no harm. Aim to keep your urine pale — but don’t force litres if it’s uncomfortable.

My relative has become more confused — isn’t that the infection?

It’s a really common assumption, but a positive urine test on its own usually isn’t the cause of confusion, and jumping to antibiotics can mean the real reason gets missed. The safer approach is to look properly for other causes — dehydration, constipation, a new medicine, being unwell in some other way — rather than blame the urine.

Why: Elderly-family scenario is central to this presentation. The teaching point from workup: look hard for a non-urinary cause of confusion before attributing it to bacteriuria.
When it WOULD need treating

So is there ever a time you would treat it?

Yes — a few clear ones. If you develop genuine urinary symptoms or a fever, that’s now a real infection and we treat it. And there are two situations where we treat even without symptoms: pregnancy, and before certain urology operations. Outside those, watchful waiting is right.

Why: Give the full “what changes my mind” list in one place — new symptoms/fever PLUS the two silent exceptions — so the plan has clear edges rather than sounding like a blanket refusal.

Why is it different in pregnancy?

In pregnancy the balance flips: even without symptoms, bacteria in the urine are more likely to cause a kidney infection and can bring on early labour. So in pregnancy we do screen for it and we do treat it — the benefit clearly outweighs the downsides there.

Why treat it before an operation?

If you’re having a urology procedure that breaks the lining of the urinary tract, we clear the bacteria first so we’re not pushing them into the bloodstream during the op. That’s a specific, one-off reason — it doesn’t mean the bacteria were harming you day to day.

I’m having another operation — do I need it treated for that?

For operations that aren’t on your urinary tract, no — there’s no benefit in treating this beforehand, and the same downsides apply. It’s specifically urology procedures that break the lining where we clear it first.

Why: Guards against over-generalising the surgical exception. The indication is urological instrumentation that breaches the mucosa, not any surgery.
When to come straight back

What symptoms mean I should come back and get it treated?

Come back if you get genuine urinary symptoms — burning or stinging when you go, needing to rush or go far more often, pain low down or in your back, or blood in the urine. And come straight back, more urgently, if you get a fever, shivering or shakes, or feel suddenly very unwell — that’s the picture of a real infection that does need antibiotics.

Why: The safety-net is the most important thing they leave with, and the thing that makes non-treatment safe. Split it: new urinary symptoms → get seen; fever/systemic → seen urgently. Say it plainly, every time.

I have a catheter — does that change things?

With a catheter there’ll almost always be some bacteria in the urine, and that alone still isn’t treated — antibiotics won’t clear it while the catheter’s in, they just cause resistance. But if you get a fever, the shivers or feel unwell, come in — that can be a real infection, and we’d treat it with antibiotics and usually change the catheter.
