Three Geometries of Failure

Where a patient feels the problem is often not where the problem is. This page gives you one question to ask first, before you think about which disease it could be: what shape of structure has failed?

What you'll be able to do
  • Sort a body structure into one of three shapes: layers, conduits or networks.
  • Ask the question that shape needs: which depth, which station, or which territory.
  • Use the answer to pick where to look next on exam and imaging.

This is a memory aid for localising a problem. It sits on top of the usual history, exam and differential diagnosis; it doesn't replace them.

The symptom shows up where the body can feel or notice it. The cause sits wherever the structure actually broke. The shape of the structure tells you how to get from one to the other.

1

Layers

The question: which depth?
Everyday picture

A wall in a house: paint, plaster, drywall, insulation, studs. A damp patch shows on the paint, but the leak is in a pipe behind the drywall. Repainting won't fix it. You have to find the layer where it starts.

In the body

The abdominal wall, skin, the meninges around the brain, the wall of the bowel or bladder, the eye's cornea. Anywhere tissue is stacked in sheets.

How to find the problem
  1. Start at the symptom on the surface.
  2. Go one layer deeper and ask: is this layer normal?
  3. Keep going until you reach the first layer that isn't. That's the lesion.
  4. Treat at that depth, not at the surface.

Worked case: indirect inguinal hernia

  1. A 45-year-old man has a groin bulge that appears when he coughs or lifts.
  2. The skin over it is normal, so the problem is deeper than the skin.
  3. The bulge follows the inguinal canal toward the scrotum, which points to the deep inguinal ring, an opening in the transversalis fascia.
  4. Repair reinforces that deep layer (usually with mesh). Nothing is done to the skin except the incision.

Common trap: treating the layer you can see. A skin ointment for a bulge, or a cream for a wound that's really tunnelling deeper, leaves the real defect untouched.

2

Conduits

The question: which station?
Everyday picture

A kitchen sink. If the pipe under it clogs, the sink fills up (above the clog) and nothing reaches the drain (below it). You don't fix it by scrubbing the sink. You find the clog, and the full sink tells you it's somewhere below.

In the body

The urinary tract, the bile ducts, the gut, the airway, the flow of fluid around the brain. Anything with a start, a series of steps, and an exit.

How to find the problem
  1. Find where the flow visibly stops (no output, a full bladder, a swollen kidney on ultrasound).
  2. Look upstream: those stations are swollen from back-pressure.
  3. Look downstream: those stations are empty or quiet.
  4. The lesion is at the boundary between swollen and empty. Treat there.

Worked case: a stone in the ureter

  1. A 38-year-old has waves of severe flank pain spreading to the groin.
  2. Ultrasound shows a swollen kidney (station 1 distended), so the block is below the kidney.
  3. CT shows a dilated ureter down to a 6 mm stone where it enters the bladder, and a normal ureter below it. Station 2 is the lesion.
  4. Treatment is aimed at the stone: let it pass, or remove it with a scope. The kidney recovers once urine can flow again.

Common trap: treating the swollen station. The kidney hurts and looks abnormal, but it's the victim. The cause is the first station where flow stopped.

3

Networks

The question: which territory?
Everyday picture

The electrical circuits in a house. When one breaker trips, one room goes dark and the rest stay on. The dark room tells you which breaker it was. You read the problem from the pattern of what's missing.

In the body

Arteries and veins, nerves, the lymphatics, the branching airways. Anything where one source fans out to supply many separate areas.

How to find the problem
  1. Map exactly what's missing: which muscles are weak, which skin is numb, which part is cold or pale.
  2. Ask which single branch supplies all of that, and nothing else.
  3. The lesion is at that branch point, upstream of the whole affected area.
  4. Treat the branch: reopen it, or protect what it supplies while collaterals take over.

Worked case: a stroke

  1. A 72-year-old suddenly can't lift their right arm, the right side of the face droops, and they can't find words.
  2. Right face, right arm and language together is one territory: the left side of the brain supplied by the left middle cerebral artery.
  3. The legs and vision are spared, which fits one branch rather than the whole brain.
  4. CT angiography confirms a blocked left middle cerebral artery. Treatment aims to reopen it, and every minute counts.

Common trap: examining the part that stopped working. The weak arm is normal; the territory map is what points you to the brain.

Most organs mix the shapes

Real organs combine all three. Naming which shape a problem belongs to tells you which question to ask about it.

Kidney

Network a clot in a renal artery branch kills a wedge of kidney. Conduit a stone blocks the outflow. Layers in some kidney diseases the filter itself leaks protein, at one layer of its wall.

Lung

Conduit a mucus plug blocks an airway. Network a pulmonary embolus cuts off a branch of the blood supply. Layers pneumonia fills the air sacs.

Skin

Layers a burn or a pressure injury is graded by how deep it goes. Network red streaks running up a limb follow the lymphatic channels.

Try it

Name the shape and the question before you open each answer.

1A 74-year-old man hasn't passed urine for 10 hours. His bladder is palpable up to his umbilicus.Show
Conduit · which station?

The bladder (station 3) is full, so the block is below it: the outlet (station 5), most often an enlarged prostate. A catheter bypasses the block.

2Two burns side by side: one is red, blistered and very painful; the other is white, leathery and painless.Show
Layers · which depth?

The painful one is partial thickness: the nerve endings in the dermis still work. The painless one has gone through the full thickness of the skin and destroyed them, so it is deeper and more serious.

3After an elbow injury, the little finger and half the ring finger are numb, and finger-spreading is weak.Show
Network · which territory?

That exact sensory and muscle pattern is the territory of the ulnar nerve, which runs behind the elbow. The lesion is at the elbow, not in the hand.

4Yellow skin and eyes, dark urine, pale stools. Ultrasound shows widened bile ducts inside the liver.Show
Conduit · which station?

Ducts are swollen above a block, and pale stools mean bile isn't reaching the gut below it. Look at the common bile duct for a stone or a tumour.

5A cold, pale left foot and calf. The femoral pulse at the groin is normal; there are no pulses below the knee.Show
Network · which territory?

Normal above, missing below: the blocked branch sits between the groin and the knee, likely the femoral or popliteal artery. This is an emergency.

6A painful bulge near an old abdominal surgery scar, worse when standing.Show
Layers · which depth?

An incisional hernia: the skin healed, but the fascia underneath didn't hold. The repair is at the fascia.

Where this model stops working

  • Whole-body problems. Sepsis, diabetes, low sodium or a drug side effect don't live at one depth, station or branch. Use the usual approach for those.
  • It finds the place, not the cause. Knowing the block is in the ureter doesn't tell you it's a stone rather than a tumour. That's the next step.
  • Organs mix shapes. If one shape doesn't explain everything you see, try another, or two together.
  • It's a teaching frame. Clinicians already localise this way, especially in neurology. This page just names the three patterns so they are easier to learn.

Words used on this page

Lesion
The spot where the structure is damaged or blocked.
Fascia
A tough sheet of connective tissue; the transversalis fascia lines the inside of the abdominal wall.
Hydronephrosis
A kidney swollen with urine that can't drain.
Ureter
The tube that carries urine from each kidney to the bladder.
Territory
The area of tissue that one artery or nerve branch supplies.
Collaterals
Back-up vessels that can take over some of a blocked branch's job.
