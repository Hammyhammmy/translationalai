Stones in Pregnancy

Renal colic across all 40 weeks: what to do in each trimester, which intervention is preferred, and how strong the evidence behind it is.

Index case · 24 weeks, healthy Observe first. If she fails, definitive URS is the preferred intervention.

Mid-second trimester is the best window for anaesthesia and surgery. URS avoids four to six months of stent exchanges. Two things switch the answer to drainage only: fever or an unconfirmed stone.

03

Decision path

Confirm the stone.

Ultrasound first, with resistive index and ureteral jets. Physiological hydronephrosis affects most pregnant women by the third trimester, right more than left. If unclear, use non-contrast MRU. Low-dose CT is a third-line option; fetal doses under 50 mGy are not associated with harm.

Screen for infection.

Fever, rising WBC, or positive urine with obstruction means drainage only: stent or PCN, antibiotics, and no definitive lithotripsy. Stent and PCN have similar obstetric outcomes, but stents had fewer UTIs, admissions, ED visits and exchanges (Mason 2023). PCN avoids general anaesthesia; a stent avoids an external tube.

Trial of observation.

Acetaminophen and opioids, hydration, and tamsulosin if accepted. About half to four-fifths of stones pass spontaneously; in one series of confirmed stones only 48% passed (Burgess 2011).

Indications to intervene.

Refractory pain, AKI or solitary kidney, persistent obstruction, contractions or preterm labour attributed to colic, or failed observation.

Choose by trimester and stone.

T2, afebrile, and a stone under about 1 cm in the ureter: URS. T1 or over 34 weeks, a large or renal stone, or limited endourology support: stent or PCN, with definitive treatment postpartum.

Radiation and MRI: counselling anxious families

Families tend to overestimate the risk of both modalities. The useful frame is that the dose ranges in stone care sit well below where harm has been shown, while an untreated obstructed or infected kidney is a known risk to the pregnancy.

Radiation: the two risks
  • Deterministic (malformation, growth restriction, IQ loss): threshold effects. No anomalies, growth restriction or loss reported below 50 mGy (ACOG). Malformations are unlikely below 100–200 mGy (ICRP 84) and arise during organogenesis. CNS/IQ effects are most sensitive at 8–15 weeks, where ACOG cites a possible minimum threshold of 60–310 mGy. At 24 weeks the threshold for CNS effects is higher still.
  • Stochastic (childhood cancer): no threshold, but tiny at these doses. Baseline childhood cancer risk is roughly 0.2–0.3%. Estimates add about 0.06% per 10 mGy (ICRP 84). A low-dose CT moves that by a few hundredths of a percent.
  • Shielding: fetal dose from CT and fluoroscopy comes mostly from internal scatter. Current physics guidance (AAPM 2019) no longer recommends routine fetal shielding in diagnostic imaging. Collimation, pulsed low-frame fluoro and short screening time matter more. Note that the AAPM statement covers diagnostic imaging; CUA (2015) still advises a lead shield between the fluoroscopy source and the fetus during URS.
MRI: what the data show
  • Non-contrast MRI: no known fetal harm at 1.5 T in any trimester. ACR supports MRI up to 3 T in Normal Operating Mode when there is expected benefit and no other practical way to get the information.
  • Ontario cohort (Ray et al., JAMA 2016, over 1.4 million births): first-trimester MRI was not associated with stillbirth, neonatal death, anomalies, cancer, or vision or hearing loss.
  • Gadolinium was associated with stillbirth or neonatal death and with inflammatory skin conditions in the same study. MRU for stones does not need it. Specify non-contrast T2 (HASTE) on the requisition.
  • Theoretical concerns are tissue heating and acoustic noise. Neither has shown harm at 1.5 T. Many centres prefer 1.5 T to limit heating (a local preference, not a guideline rule), and use left lateral tilt after about 20 weeks.

Words that work at the bedside

Radiation

We keep X-ray time during the procedure as short as possible. Doctors have only seen harm at doses many times higher than a short X-ray run gives.

If we need a CT, we use a low-dose stone protocol. It keeps the baby's dose well within the range that has been studied and found safe.

MRI

MRI uses magnets, not radiation. We will not use contrast dye. Large studies, including one from Ontario, found no effect on babies.

Framing the trade-off

A blocked or infected kidney is a bigger risk to the pregnancy than any of these tests. Imaging lets us treat the right problem instead of guessing.

Dose ranges are approximate literature values. Your department's medical physicist can give site-specific fetal dose estimates, which families often find reassuring in writing.

07

Drugs and the OR

Analgesia and MET
  • Acetaminophen first line
  • Opioids short course; warn about neonatal effects near term
  • NSAIDs avoided from 20 weeks (FDA). Health Canada: do not use from around 20 to 28 weeks unless advised, lowest dose for shortest time if needed; contraindicated in the last trimester
  • Tamsulosin off-label; retrospective data show no adverse maternal or infant outcomes, but no proven benefit for passage
Antibiotics
  • Safe: cephalosporins, penicillins, fosfomycin
  • Avoid: fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines
  • TMP-SMX: avoid in T1 and near term
  • Nitrofurantoin: avoid at term (haemolysis risk); in T1 use only when no suitable alternative (ACOG CO 717)
Intraop
  • Left lateral tilt
  • Fluoro off or pulsed, tight collimation; ultrasound-guided wire confirmation
  • Low-pressure irrigation
  • Holmium laser; avoid EHL
Watch
  • Access sheath in an oedematous, dilated ureter
  • VTE prophylaxis per OB
  • Post-op monitoring for contractions
  • Short stent on a string; CUA recommends a stent after URS in pregnancy
08

Where this is fragile

  • The evidence base is thin. There are no large RCTs. The URS-over-stent preference rests on pooled case series and one small randomized comparison. EAU rates URS as a reasonable alternative at LE 1b; CUA grades its pregnancy statement LE 4, Grade C.
  • Diagnosis is the real failure point. Negative ureteroscopy happens when physiological hydro is taken for obstruction. Confirm with MRU before you go to the OR.
  • Outcomes depend on the operator. Published series come from high-volume endourology units. A low-volume operator at a site without OB and NICU backup changes the risk calculation.
  • Obstetric endpoints are under-reported. Preterm labour and fetal outcomes are inconsistently captured across series. Low event rates may partly reflect missing data.
