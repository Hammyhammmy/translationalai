The Urinary Tract — A Conduit

Urine is made in the kidney and leaves through the urethra, passing five stations on the way. It flows one way only. The patient feels the problem at the exit, but the cause is often further up. Your job is to find which part failed first.

How to think about the urinary tract

The urinary tract is a conduit: a one-way tube, like a garden hose, and the second of the three geometries. So the question is different from the abdominal wall. You don't ask "how deep is the problem?" You ask "where along the tube did the flow first stop?"

Tubes go wrong in a few typical ways. Block one and pressure builds above the block while nothing comes out the end. Let bacteria in and the infection travels down with the flow and climbs back up against it. Damage the wall and urine leaks out where it shouldn't. The pattern tells you where the problem is.

The one rule to remember

Start where the flow visibly stops: that's where the patient notices it. Then work back up the tube. Everything above the block shows damage from back-pressure (a widened ureter, a swollen bladder, an injured kidney). Everything below is empty or quiet (nothing coming out, shrunken from disuse). The problem sits where the swelling ends and the quiet begins.

The five stations, kidney to outlet

They are numbered in the direction urine flows: the kidney makes it, the outlet lets it out. Each part has one job and a typical way of going wrong.

5 stations · urine flows one way · work back up from where it stops
1
Production · kidney The glomerulus filters the blood. The tubules concentrate what's filtered. The collecting ducts deliver urine to the calyces and the renal pelvis, the kidney's own drainage system.
When it goes wrong: Blood in the urine (hematuria) from the filter or kidney tissue. Kidney infection (pyelonephritis). Kidney masses. Acute kidney injury (AKI) from prerenal, intrarenal or postrenal causes. A swollen kidney (hydronephrosis) when the ureter is blocked.
2
Transport · ureter A 25 cm muscular tube. Waves of muscle contraction (peristalsis) push urine along, even against gravity. It narrows naturally in three places where stones often stick: where it leaves the kidney (ureteropelvic junction, UPJ), where it crosses the pelvic brim, and where it enters the bladder (ureterovesical junction, UVJ).
When it goes wrong: A stone blocks it (colicky pain, with a swollen kidney above). Narrowing (stricture) after surgery or an old stone. Pressure from outside (a mass, retroperitoneal fibrosis). Urine flowing back up (reflux) if the one-way valve at the UVJ doesn't close.
3
Storage · bladder A stretchy reservoir. Its most important property is that it holds urine at low pressure. The trigone (the base of the bladder) is rich in sensory nerves. The bladder muscle (detrusor) contracts on command to empty it.
When it goes wrong: Two patterns: it can't store (urgency, frequency, an overactive bladder) or it can't empty (retention, a high post-void residual or PVR, the urine left after voiding). High pressure while storing pushes urine back up and damages the ureters and kidneys.
4
Control · sphincters & nerves Two gates. The internal sphincter (smooth muscle, sympathetic α1, not under conscious control) sits at the bladder neck. The external sphincter (striated muscle, pudendal nerve, under conscious control) sits at the membranous urethra. The pons in the brainstem coordinates them.
When it goes wrong: Leaking (the gates won't hold). Detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (the gates won't open when the bladder squeezes). Stress incontinence when the pelvic floor doesn't support the urethra. Nerve-related (neurogenic) patterns from spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis (MS), stroke or diabetes.
5
Outlet · prostate & urethra The exit tube. In men it runs through the prostate, a solid gland wrapped around the tube. Narrowing, squeezing and blockage all happen here.
When it goes wrong: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH): the enlarged prostate squeezes the urethra from outside. Stricture after infection, instruments (catheters, scopes) or trauma. A narrowed opening (meatal stenosis). Tumour. A block here pushes back-pressure all the way up to the kidneys.

Inside the kidney: where the problem sits decides the disease

For the patient, this means two people with "a kidney problem" can need completely different doctors. The kidney is the first stop of the tract, but inside it urine also flows along its own short path, from filter to drain. It's worth one close look, because problems inside the kidney behave differently from problems anywhere else in the tract.

urine flows one way inside the kidney too · where it fails decides the disease

Where the problem sits inside the kidney changes the disease completely. A problem in the glomerulus is one kind of disease (protein in the urine, nephritic syndromes). A problem in the tubules is another (the kidney can't concentrate urine, acute kidney injury). A problem in the collecting ducts, calyx or pelvis is a urology problem (stones in a calyx, a blockage where the pelvis meets the ureter at the UPJ). Same organ, three places, three different specialties.

How the three geometries meet in the kidney

Seen from the urinary tract, the kidney is the first stop of a tube. Inside, it is a filter made of nephrons stacked side by side (layers), fed by a branching tree of blood vessels (a network). That is why a kidney can fail in three different ways: disease of the kidney tissue (a layer problem), a blood-supply event (a network problem, e.g. a renal infarct), and a blocked drainage system (a conduit problem, e.g. a stone). Each has a different specialist, a different test and a different fix.

Filling and emptying: the two phases of the bladder

For the patient, this is why "I leak" and "I can't go" are different problems. The bladder (station 3) and its control gates, the sphincters and nerves (station 4), work as a team. At any moment they are either filling or emptying, never both. A centre in the brainstem, the pontine micturition centre, switches between the two.

PhaseBladder muscle (detrusor)Internal sphincterExternal sphincter
STORAGE

VOIDING

Three things have to be true for voiding to work: the bladder muscle has to squeeze hard enough, both sphincters have to open, and the kidneys and ureters have to have delivered urine to empty. If the timing is lost, and the bladder squeezes against a closed sphincter (dyssynergia), you get high pressure and no flow. To the kidneys, that pressure is the same as a blocked bladder outlet, and it damages the ureters and kidneys in the same way.

What keeps the bladder working normally

  • Low pressure while filling. The bladder muscle stretches to hold >400 mL with almost no rise in pressure. High pressure while filling is abnormal: it pushes back up the ureters and damages the kidneys.
  • Sphincters and bladder in step. When the bladder muscle squeezes, the sphincters open. Lose this and the patient pushes at high pressure with no flow.
  • Feeling matches volume. First urge at ~150 mL, strong urge at ~300 mL, capacity ~400–500 mL. If the feeling doesn't match the volume, suspect a problem with the bladder's sensory nerves.
A simpler way to sort symptoms

Most bladder and outlet symptoms come from one of three problems: storing (urgency, frequency, leaking: the bladder), emptying (hesitancy, weak stream, retention: the control gates or the outlet), or timing (dribbling after voiding, high-pressure low-flow: the control gates and nerves). Pick the group first. The list of possible causes shrinks straight away.

Four ways the urinary tract goes wrong

Four kinds of problem explain almost everything. For each one: where the problem actually is, where the patient notices it, and what the back-pressure above it looks like.

stone, BPH, stricture, tumor, clot		 Wherever the tube narrows (most often the ureter or the outlet) Nothing or little comes out (weak stream, no urine), or pain where the tube is swollen above the block Swelling above the block: a swollen kidney (hydronephrosis) if the ureter is blocked, a swollen bladder if the outlet is

renal cell carcinoma (RCC), urothelial cancer, prostate cancer		 Any part; depends on the tissue Blood in the urine, or found by chance with no symptoms Often nothing above it until the tumor blocks the tube. Painless visible blood in the urine is cancer until proven otherwise, precisely because there is no back-pressure to warn you.
The trap

"Burning when I pee" is felt at the outlet (station 5). The reflex is to treat it as a bladder infection (cystitis). But the same symptom comes from urethritis (the urethra), prostatitis (the prostate, next to the urethra), a low stone stuck at the UVJ (where the ureter meets the bladder), or atrophic vaginitis next door. The urinalysis (UA) isn't there to confirm a UTI; it tells you where to look. A clean UA in a "UTI" means the bladder is probably not the problem, and you need to look elsewhere.

Choosing a test: which part can it see?

For the patient, this means fewer and better-chosen tests. Each test shows a different part of the tract. Don't choose a test from the symptom; choose it from the part you still can't see. Know what each test shows, and just as important, what it misses.

The pattern

Tests get more invasive the further inside they look. A UA is cheap and only reads the urine. Cystoscopy looks directly inside and is invasive. A biopsy takes tissue and can't be undone. Move to a deeper test only when a simpler one hasn't told you where the problem is.

When a senior asks "what's the next test?" they are really asking "which part can't you see yet?" Answer that and the test picks itself.

Treat where the problem is, not where it hurts

This is what separates thinking about the whole tube from just treating symptoms. The temptation is to treat where the patient is uncomfortable: at the exit. The right move is to treat where the flow first stopped.

BPH: the classic example

An enlarged prostate squeezes the urethra that runs through it (the outlet, station 5). The patient comes in with retention: the bladder can't empty, and the complaint is at the exit ("I can't go"). The bladder isn't the cause. It has been pushing against an outlet that won't open. Pressure builds back up through the ureters to the kidneys, and the kidneys pay the price.

A catheter fixes the exit: urine comes out. But the prostate is still there. Take the catheter out and the retention comes back. The problem is at the outlet. The treatments all act there:

  • α-blocker (tamsulosin): relaxes smooth muscle at the bladder neck and in the prostatic urethra. Acts on the control gates and outlet.
  • 5α-reductase inhibitor (finasteride): shrinks the prostate over months. Acts on the gland itself.
  • TURP / HoLEP / UroLift (transurethral resection of the prostate, holmium laser enucleation of the prostate, prostatic urethral lift): remove or pull back the blocking tissue with surgery. The lasting fix at the outlet.

A catheter treats the symptom. Each of the above treats the cause. Both are sometimes right, but you should always know which one you are doing and why.

The habit to build

"Stop the bleeding, stop the pain, drain the blockage" is emergency care. It buys time. It is not the fix. Once the patient is stable, ask every time: where did the flow first stop, and what treats it there? Then plan the lasting treatment at that spot.

A Worked Case — "I Can't Pee"

A 68-year-old man comes to the emergency department (ED). His lower belly feels full, he hasn't urinated in 14 hours, and his last try produced "a few drops." Work back up the tract.

1
The complaint at the exit · outlet
"I can't pee."
Flow has visibly stopped at the exit. You don't yet know which part failed first. On exam the lower belly is full: the bladder can be felt, and it sounds dull when tapped above the pubic bone. He notices it at the outlet. The cause could be at the bladder or anywhere below it. The bladder isn't emptying, but you don't know why yet.
Decision: bladder scan before anything else.
2
Find where the urine is backed up · bladder
820 mL retained.
This is acute retention. The bladder can hold urine: its storing works, and is in fact overworked. Pressure is building back from the outlet. The problem is either below the bladder muscle or in the nerves that sense fullness. Keep looking.
Decision: drain the bladder (Foley catheter) and keep looking for the cause at the same time. Don't lose the chance to find it.
3
Check the urine · UA
No leukocytes, no nitrites, no blood, trace protein.
A clean UA. A bladder infection (cystitis) isn't driving this. No blood, so no obvious sign of cancer yet. The simplest test has already crossed causes off the list.
Off the list: retention caused by a UTI.
4
Check the wiring · nerves (control)
No back pain, no saddle anesthesia, no leg weakness, no recent neurologic change. Diabetic but well-controlled, no neuropathy on exam. Not on anticholinergics, decongestants, or opioids.
Nerve and medication causes drop off the list. Cauda equina syndrome isn't the cause, but you still asked, because you'd never forgive yourself for putting in a catheter and sending home a man whose spinal cord was being squeezed.
Off the list: nerve-related retention, drug-induced retention.
5
Focused history · bladder & outlet
Slow stream "for years." Wakes 3× nightly. Hesitancy. Felt "incomplete" emptying for months. No prior catheterization, no instrumentation, no STIs, no trauma.
Months of the outlet slowly narrowing, and now it has suddenly given out. A stricture is unlikely without past instruments or infection. This fits an enlarged prostate squeezing the urethra that runs through it. Nothing is stuck inside the urethra; its wall is being squeezed from outside.
Working diagnosis: BPH causing a long-standing outlet blockage that has now suddenly worsened.
6
Feel the problem directly · digital rectal exam (DRE)
Prostate symmetrically enlarged, smooth, rubbery, non-tender. No nodules.
Fits benign enlargement. No nodule means no immediate suspicion of prostate cancer on this exam. That doesn't rule it out, and a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) test still belongs in the workup once the bladder is drained (acute retention itself raises PSA, so timing matters). Not tender, so prostatitis is off the list.
Off the list: acute prostatitis, prostate cancer you can feel.
7
Check for damage higher up · kidneys and ureters
Creatinine 1.4 (baseline 1.0). Mild bilateral hydronephrosis.
The problem is at the outlet, but back-pressure has reached the kidneys. High pressure in the bladder has pushed back through the bladder neck, the bladder and the ureters into the kidneys. This is why you don't treat retention as an exit problem and walk away. The kidneys are paying the price. Draining the bladder protects the kidneys; it isn't just for comfort.
What follows: recheck creatinine after draining. Expect heavy urine output once the blockage is relieved (post-obstructive diuresis); watch for it.
8
Problem found · outlet
Acute urinary retention from BPH. The prostate is squeezing the urethra at the outlet. Kidneys and ureters mildly affected; kidney function should recover.
Five stations, eight steps, one cause. Notice where you stopped: you didn't need urodynamics, cystoscopy or a CT. Those come next if the first treatment fails. You tested only as far as you needed to be sure enough to act.
Treat at the outlet.
Rx
Treatment · step by step
Drain now. Treat the cause next. Plan the lasting fix.
  1. Foley catheter: relieves the back-pressure all the way up. Treats the symptom. Buys time.
  2. Tamsulosin: relaxes the smooth muscle at the bladder neck and prostate. Started right away to improve the odds of passing a voiding trial.
  3. Voiding trial in 3–7 days: catheter out, to see whether the α-blocker alone lets him void.
  4. Outpatient urology follow-up: PSA (delayed), and a discussion of a 5α-reductase inhibitor (5-ARI) vs. surgery (TURP / HoLEP / UroLift). This is where the cause is actually treated.
  5. Recheck kidney function: confirm the kidneys recover once the outlet is opened.
The Foley is not the treatment. It is the bridge to the treatment.
What this case teaches

The patient pointed at the exit. The bladder scan showed the bladder was swollen. The cause was the prostate at the outlet. The back-pressure had reached the kidneys. Every part mattered, but only one needed the definitive treatment. Ask of every patient: where did the flow first stop, and what treats it there?

Where it hurts vs. where the problem is: referred pain

For the patient, this means the spot they point to is often not where the problem is. The tube is damaged in one place, but the pain is felt in the patch of skin that shares its nerve supply (the dermatome). Trust the patient's pointing finger and you'll often be wrong about location.

  • Stone at the UPJ (where the kidney meets the ureter) → flank pain from the kidney's capsule being stretched. Mimics musculoskeletal (MSK) back pain.
  • Stone in the middle of the ureter → pain in the side of the abdomen. Mimics appendicitis on the right, diverticulitis on the left.
  • Stone at the UVJ (where the ureter meets the bladder) → groin / testicular / labial pain. Mimics hernia, torsion, epididymitis.
  • Bladder problems → pain above the pubic bone, sometimes felt at the tip of the penis or in the perineum.
  • Prostatitis (the prostate, around the urethra) → perineum, low back, tip of penis, testicles, rectum. Five places from one inflamed gland.
  • Kidney capsule stretch (the kidney) → flank, costovertebral angle. May spread to the shoulder on the same side if the diaphragm is irritated.

The pattern: the patient's finger points to the dermatome, not the organ. It's the same lesson anywhere referred pain shows up. Believe the symptom, doubt the location, and image the part you suspect.

The steps, in order

Work back up from where the flow stops
Step 1 — Take the complaint at face value.
  → Patient says "burning," "blood," "can't go," "leaking."
  → That names the group: storing / emptying / blood / pain.

Step 2 — Start with the simplest test that rules out the most.
  → UA + culture. Bladder scan if the problem is emptying.
  → A clean UA in a "UTI" changes your path. So does a 600 mL PVR.

Step 3 — Let the history point to the part.
  → Nerve symptoms?        → outside the tract · the nerves (control)
  → Slow stream for years? → long-standing outlet block · prostate/urethra
  → Painless visible blood? → assume cancer until imaged + scoped

Step 4 — Examine the suspected part directly when you can.
  → DRE for the prostate. Look at the urethral opening (meatus).
  → Pelvic exam where appropriate.

Step 5 — Image the part you still can't see.
  → Kidney or ureter?     → CT urogram / renal ultrasound
  → How it works?         → urodynamics
  → Inside the tube?      → cystoscopy

Step 6 — Take tissue only when imaging can't answer.
  → Biopsy is the most invasive test. Use it last, and aim it.

Step 7 — Treat where the problem is.
  → Not where it hurts. Not at the exit. Not at the swelling above.
  → At the place where the flow first stopped.

Words used on this page

Conduit
A one-way tube. Here, the whole path from kidney to outlet.
Station
One of the five parts of the tract: kidney (1), ureter (2), bladder (3), control: sphincters, pelvic floor and nerves (4), outlet: prostate and urethra (5).
Lesion
The spot where the tract is damaged or blocked.
Detrusor
The muscle in the bladder wall that squeezes to empty it.
Sphincter
A ring of muscle that holds urine in until it relaxes.
Hydronephrosis
A kidney swollen with urine that can't drain.
UPJ / UVJ
Where the ureter leaves the kidney (ureteropelvic junction) and where it enters the bladder (ureterovesical junction).
PVR
Post-void residual: urine left in the bladder after voiding.
Dyssynergia
The bladder squeezes while the sphincter stays shut, so pressure rises with no flow.
Gross hematuria
Blood in the urine you can see.
Dermatome
The patch of skin supplied by one spinal nerve, where referred pain is felt.
