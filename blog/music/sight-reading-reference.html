Landmarks &
Intervals

You don't decode notes one letter at a time. You anchor to a few known pitches, then ride intervals out from them. This is the whole skill.

01The anchors

Hold 3–4 reference notes per clef in permanent memory. Every other note is read relative to the nearest anchor, never counted up from the bottom.

Treble (G clef)

Every position labeled — lines in clay, spaces in ink, one continuous ladder
Lines ↑E · G · B · D · F   (Every Good Boy Deserves Fudge) Spaces ↑F · A · C · E   (spells FACE, bottom to top) InterleavedE·L F·S G·L A·S B·L C·S D·L E·S F·L — the two E's are an octave apart

Bass (F clef)

Every position labeled — same ladder, shifted down two positions from treble
Lines ↑G · B · D · F · A   (Good Boys Do Fine Always) Spaces ↑A · C · E · G   (All Cows Eat Grass) InterleavedG·L A·S B·L C·S D·L E·S F·L G·S A·L — the two G's are an octave apart
The point Mnemonics are training wheels for getting the anchors in. Once the anchors are instant, stop reciting them — fluent reading is anchor-then-interval, not spelling the sentence every time.

02Read shape, not letters

Notes a 3rd apart stack: line-on-line or space-on-space. When a triad is written in closed voicing it reads as a single shape you recognize whole — not three notes read in sequence. (When it's spread open, the shape breaks up — more on that below.)

Take F–A–C: instead of reading "F, then A, then C" note by note, learn to see the chord as one object. In closed voicing that object is a tidy stack; in open voicing it's the same three tones spread wider. Either way you're reading the chord, not spelling three separate notes.

Treble — a stacked triad reads as one object

Stacked vs. spread is voicing — but check the bass note

The tidy block is only one voicing. The same three chord tones can be spread out across the staff with gaps between them. But spreading a chord often also changes which tone ends up lowest — and that's a separate property called inversion, decided by the lowest sounding note across both staves.

Your plagal IV chord is a case where both happen at once. The tones are F–A–C (that's IV, F major), but the lowest note in the bass clef is C — the 5th of the chord, not the root. A IV chord with its 5th in the bass is second inversion (IV⁶₄). So it's spread open and inverted. The gap told you the voicing was open; the bass note is what told you it was inverted.

Same chord — F·A·C — different voicing and inversion
Two independent axes — read both Voicing is how close the notes sit: closed (tight stack) vs. open (spread with gaps). Inversion is which chord tone is lowest overall — and it's decided by the bass note, not by how the treble looks. They're independent: a chord can be closed-and-inverted or open-and-root. The plagal IV happens to be both open and inverted — F–A–C with C (the 5th) in the bass, so it's IV⁶₄. The takeaway: a gap tells you the voicing is open, but only the lowest note tells you the inversion. Always read the bass.

03Ride the intervals

Land one note from an anchor, then read every following note as distance from the last. Sight-readers almost never re-identify from scratch.

Unison — same line/space
2nd — line→adjacent space (a step)
3rd — line→line or space→space (a skip)
4th — line→space, one gap over
5th — line→line, skip a line
Octave — same letter, one line + one space away

The counting-up habit (E-G-B-D-F… from the bottom every time) is the thing that caps your speed. Kill it by reading distance, not position.

04Drills

Anatomy before physiology: get the static landmarks solid before you try to read them in motion.

  1. Landmark speed · 5 min/day
    Flashcard just the anchors (treble G/E/F, bass F/G/A) until naming is instant, no hesitation.
  2. Interval jumps · 5 min/day
    From each anchor, name the note a 3rd and a 5th away — without counting. This is where speed actually comes from.
  3. Chord-shape recognition
    Spot stacked triads as single units. You're already primed for this from theory — now make the eye do it.
  4. Rhythm alone
    Read rhythm with no pitch first — tap or clap it. Then merge with pitch. Reading both cold at once is the usual bottleneck.
  5. Daily easy sight-reading
    Always slightly below your ceiling. Hands separate. Never stop to correct.
